## Genre, structure, and Hebrew pattern

Psalm 37 is wisdom-like instruction about the apparent success of the wicked and the future of those who wait for YHWH. Its Hebrew alphabetic acrostic marks successive units across the poem. The Masoretic form has no unit beginning with the expected letter ayin (ע). The observation is visible in the Hebrew text; translated verse numbers do not display the initial-letter pattern.

The poem’s parallel lines repeatedly contrast the wicked (`rashaʿ`) and the righteous (`tsaddiq`), using imperatives, assurance, and instruction. It is not a chronological narrative of a single injustice or an unconditional prediction that all righteous people will prosper immediately.

## Scholarly disagreement and setting

Benun argues that irregularities in Psalm 37’s acrostic can be deliberate literary signposts, connecting formal disruption with disruption of moral order. This is his proposal, not consensus. Other explanations include transmission loss or a complex compositional history. The absence of a stanza alone cannot decide among them, and the CKL item does not restore conjectural Hebrew text.

No superscription dates the poem or names a setting. Its wisdom instruction plausibly addresses a community or learners confronting injustice, but it does not identify a school, monarch, or historical crisis.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 37:1–40.
- **Later Jewish reception:** no particular rabbinic interpretation of the acrostic gap is added here.
- **Later Christian reception:** Matthew 5:5 uses Greek wording corresponding to Psalm 37:11 (LXX Psalm 36:11). Domeris discusses the translation and social meaning of “meek” and the land; this is Christian reception of a related line, not evidence that Psalm 37’s acrostic gap was intentional.
