## Structure, vocabulary, and literary function

The short poem asks who may sojourn in YHWH’s tent and dwell on the holy mountain (v. 1). A series of conduct statements answers the question: integrity, justice, truthful speech, refusal to slander or harm a neighbor, and rejection of bribery (vv. 2–5). The second half is social and ethical as well as cultic; it does not describe a ritual sequence.

The Hebrew verbs translated “sojourn” and “dwell” can evoke guest residence and continued habitation. “Tent” and “holy mountain” invoke sacred presence, but the text does not locate a gate, name a pilgrimage festival, or specify who asks and answers. The parallel question and response suit instruction for a worshipping community.

## Setting and scholarly disagreement

Boloje and Groenewald classify the psalm as an entrance liturgy while emphasizing its ethical and didactic function. Their review of earlier proposals underscores that a precise cultic setting or festival cannot be recovered. “Entrance liturgy” is therefore a useful genre label, not a transcript of an identified temple rite. A community approaching YHWH is a plausible original audience; the text does not date that community or describe a particular service.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 15:1–5.
- **Later Jewish reception:** Babylonian Talmud, *Makkot* 24a, reads David’s eleven principles in Psalm 15 as a condensation of the commandments. This rabbinic enumeration is later interpretation, not the poem’s stated compositional intent.
- **Later Christian reception:** no distinct, passage-specific Christian reuse was verified for this packet. The ethical catalogue has later interpretive uses, but none is used to infer the original setting.
