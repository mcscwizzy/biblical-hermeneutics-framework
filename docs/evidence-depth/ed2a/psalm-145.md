# Psalm 145 research packet

## Decision and CKL reuse

Baseline: no eligible CKL material for Psalm 145. The Psalms object has broad notes on Qumran witnesses and ancient numbering, but no passage-scoped record for the missing nun unit. Add `psalm-145-nun-verse-witness`, scoped exactly to Psalm 145:13. It is the wave’s only manuscript evidence item.

## Literary form and textual observation

Psalm 145 is an alphabetic praise acrostic. In the Masoretic form, the mem unit at 145:13a is followed by the samekh unit at v. 14; the expected nun (נ) unit is absent. The Hebrew 11QPsᵃ witness preserves a nun-initial line after the kingdom statement, and the Greek Psalter (Psalm 144:13b) contains a corresponding line. One common translation is “God/the Lord is faithful in his words and gracious/loyal in all his works.” The Qumran Hebrew uses “God”; the Greek has “Lord”; the wording is not identical.

The Qumran copy repeats a blessing refrain at intervals in Psalm 145, and the line occurs in that manuscript’s textual form. The evidence item limits its claim to the presence of differing witnesses; it does not call the received Masoretic text corrupt.

## Manuscript dating and interpretive questions

11QPsᵃ (11Q5), the Great Psalms Scroll, is generally dated paleographically to the first century CE; the exact date is approximate. This date applies to that manuscript copy, not to the composition of Psalm 145. The Greek translation tradition is earlier than the surviving Hebrew scroll, but the time of translation for an individual psalm does not by itself establish the relative age of every reading.

Text critics have explained the Masoretic gap as a lost stanza or as a deliberate acrostic disruption. Gentry argues for loss in the proto-Masoretic line. Benun’s broader study argues that acrostic disruptions can be deliberate literary features. These views are not collapsed into a consensus. The independent witness readings establish that a nun-strophe circulated in more than one ancient textual tradition; the evidence by itself does not prove which form is authorial or why the forms differ.

## Audience and later reception

The poem is praise directed to God and framed for repeated praise; the text does not name its first performer or date. The Qumran scroll’s refrain documents a later manuscript/worship form, not the poem’s original liturgical setting.

- **Later Jewish reception:** Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 4b asks about the absent nun and gives a homiletical explanation based on Amos 5:2; the same tractate praises the repeated recitation of the psalm in daily prayer. Both are later rabbinic/liturgical reception, not explanation of the original author’s intention.
- **Later Christian reception:** the Greek Psalter tradition transmits the line, and Psalm 145 is used in Christian Psalter reading. No distinct Christian interpretation of this variant is asserted here.
