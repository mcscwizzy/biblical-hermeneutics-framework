## Genre, structure, and language

Psalm 44 is a communal lament. In common English numbering, it begins with inherited memories of divine victories for the ancestors (vv. 1–8), turns to present defeat and humiliation (vv. 9–16), protests that the community has not forgotten the covenant (vv. 17–22), and pleads for God to rise and redeem (vv. 23–26). Some Hebrew editions count the superscription as v. 1, shifting the poem’s verse numbers by one. Repeated first-person plural forms make the collective voice central. Its “we” could be voiced by a group, but the poem does not identify a particular congregation or performance rite.

Images of defeat and public shame intensify the lament. The community’s appeal to its own fidelity is not a modern historical report; it is part of a literary argument addressed to God. The poem never names a battle, invading ruler, or date.

## Historical proposals and limits

Verde argues that communal laments in Psalter Books II–III helped shape a Persian-period Yehud memory of the Babylonian catastrophe, whether through composition, redaction, or both. He explicitly notes that the dates and events behind Psalm 44 and related laments are disputed. This supports a proposal about later book-level framing and use; it does not prove that Psalm 44 was first written after 587 BCE or that it recounts one identifiable battle.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 44:1–26.
- **Later Jewish reception:** no additional passage-specific rabbinic interpretation is required for this item.
- **Later Christian reception:** Romans 8:36 quotes Psalm 44:23 (“for your sake we are being killed all day long” in common English rendering) in Paul’s argument about suffering and assurance. This Christian reuse has its own context; it does not identify Psalm 44’s original defeat.
