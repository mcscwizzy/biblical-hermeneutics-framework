## Genre, structure, Hebrew, and audience

Psalm 96 opens with a call to sing a “new song” and to tell the nations of YHWH’s glory (vv. 1–3). It contrasts YHWH with the peoples’ `ʾĕlîlîm` (often translated “idols” or “worthless gods”; v. 5), summons nations and creation to worship (vv. 7–9), declares “YHWH reigns” (v. 10), and closes with creation’s rejoicing because YHWH comes to judge with equity (vv. 11–13). The poem’s addressees are expansive and plural. Its universal language is not a report that all nations had joined Israelite worship.

## History and interpretation

The psalm belongs to the YHWH-kingship hymn group (Pss 93–100). Hays argues that its exuberant song can hold memories of oppression, injustice, and divine absence while supporting resilience. She says explicitly that the psalm is not about trauma and does not respond to a recoverable acute event. Her reading is a modern interpretive proposal, not a consensus dating method.

No date or historical rebuilding project is named in the Hebrew poem. The Hebrew Bible later places closely parallel material in David’s narrative in 1 Chronicles 16:23–33. The Greek superscription to Psalm 95 (MT Psalm 96) frames the song “when the house was built after the captivity.” These are later textual/canonical frames; they do not prove the original composition setting.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 96:1–13.
- **Later Jewish textual/canonical reception:** 1 Chronicles 16:23–33 reuses related psalmic material; direction and composition history should not be inferred from the parallel alone.
- **Later Greek reception:** Rahlfs–Hanhart records the Psalm 95 superscription in the Septuagint. It is not part of the Hebrew poem’s superscription.
- **Later Christian reception:** the psalm circulates in Christian use of the Psalter; no distinct Christian reinterpretation is needed for this item, and none is used to reconstruct the poem’s original audience.
