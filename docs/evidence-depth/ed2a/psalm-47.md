## Literary movement and Hebrew

In common English numbering, the short hymn calls peoples to clap (v. 1), marks God’s ascent with a shout and trumpet (v. 5), and summons repeated song (vv. 6–7). It grounds praise in God’s rule over peoples and nations (vv. 2–4), then declares God king over the nations and seated on a holy throne (vv. 7–9). The refrain of praise and repeated imperatives give the poem a communal performance quality. Its royal vocabulary is explicit; the specific festival is not. The superscription is not counted as a numbered verse in this scope.

## Genre, setting, and scholarly disagreement

Perdue classifies Psalm 47 with the YHWH-kingship / enthronement hymns. His article surveys competing “situation in life” reconstructions and the major annual enthronement or New Year festival theory. He also reports criticism of arguments from external festival analogies and late sources. The poem may have been performed by a gathered worshipping community, but an annual royal ceremony, a particular monarch, and a historical victory are not established by the text.

The trumpet/shofar language and divine kingship later made the psalm useful in Jewish Rosh Hashanah liturgy. That later use cannot be projected backward as proof of the original occasion. No divine council scene is needed to explain the text’s reference to God’s throne and the nations.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 47:1–9.
- **Later Jewish reception:** later Jewish liturgical practice recites Psalm 47 before the shofar blasts on Rosh Hashanah; *Arukh ha-Shulḥan*, *Orach Chayim* 585:4, attests the sevenfold recitation custom. The practice postdates the poem.
- **Later Christian reception:** no direct New Testament quotation or distinct later Christian reading was verified for this packet.
