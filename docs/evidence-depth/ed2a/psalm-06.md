## Literary movement, language, and audience

Psalm 6 is commonly classified as an individual lament or prayer for help. In common English numbering, its movement runs from a plea against rebuke and bodily distress (vv. 1–3), through an intensifying plea and description of grief (vv. 4–7), to confidence that YHWH has heard the plea and will answer (vv. 8–10). The superscription is not counted as a numbered verse in this scope. The precise boundary between plea, complaint, and confidence is a matter of form-critical description, not evidence for a named ceremony.

The poem uses `ʿăṣāmîm` (“bones”) and `nephesh` (“life/self,” often rendered “soul”) alongside crying, a troubled eye, and an adversarial setting. These are embodied poetic expressions of distress. Wessels and Coetzee analyze the body as rhetoric across several Psalm genres, including Psalm 6; they do not establish a clinical diagnosis. Translation choices can make the body/self relation sound more sharply divided than the Hebrew requires.

The first-person singular makes an individual worshipper a plausible speaker or performer. The text does not identify the speaker, a medical cause, a temple rite, or a historical audience beyond a worship setting that remains possible.

## Ancient sources and reception

- **Primary source:** Masoretic Psalm 6:2–8.
- **Later Jewish reception:** no passage-specific Jewish reinterpretation was added in this wave.
- **Later Christian reception:** Psalm 6 became the first of the traditional Seven Penitential Psalms. Brozinčević traces the group as a late-antique Christian reception, with Cassiodorus’ sixth-century commentary as the first clear historical witness to these seven as a fixed unit; this classification is not Psalm 6’s original genre.
