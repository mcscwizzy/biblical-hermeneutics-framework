## Proverbs 2 — conditional argument structure

- **Existing CKL material:** The chapter had contextual/background material but no eligible passage-anchored claim, typed evidence item, or passage-specific interpretive note.
- **Research question:** How does the conditional syntax organize the poem, and where does its argument reach its conclusion?
- **Verified source:** J. A. Loader, “Confusing redaction and corruption: A house going to hell,” *Verbum et Ecclesia* 34, no. 2 (2013), article 797. The full open-access HTML was inspected, especially “Proverbs 2:18 and the organisation of its context.”
- **Textual observation:** Loader analyzes the opening as a three-part protasis (vv. 1–4), followed by two consequence units (vv. 5–8 and 9–22).
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Loader argues that v. 20 is the capstone for the whole conditional argument. He regards the proposed break after v. 11 as weak and notes an alternative division associated with Fox. This is an attributed structural proposal, not a settled outline.
- **Original-context limit:** The poem’s “my son” address is a literary teaching voice. This evidence does not establish a real classroom, specific teacher, or composition date.
- **Record:** `proverbs-2-conditional-argument-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how the warnings about crooked paths and the closing way of the upright fit within a continuing argument.

## Proverbs 28 — sayings about rulers and the poor

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-anchored material was present for this chapter.
- **Research question:** Does Proverbs 28’s concern with rulers, justice, and the poor participate in a larger literary sequence?
- **Verified source:** Maurice Gilbert, “Les conditions du pouvoir: Le regard des sages sur la classe dirigeante (Pr 28-29),” *Revue Théologique de Louvain* 38, no. 3 (2007), 313–335, DOI 10.2143/RTL.38.3.2022850. The publisher’s English abstract and the opening page in Persée were inspected; the complete argument was not available in readable text.
- **Textual observation:** Gilbert treats Proverbs 28–29 as a literary/thematic unit and identifies a frame at 28:1, 12, 28 and 29:15, 27 around four intervening pericopes.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He reads the unit as wisdom about the ruling class and emphasizes concern for poor people under oppressive or corrupt power. The record is limited to the author’s abstract-level proposal and does not assign the sayings to a specific crisis or ruler.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** The full article argument and its detailed analysis could not be checked. The evidence remains low-confidence and requires full-text and human review.
- **Record:** `proverbs-28-rulers-and-poor-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Offers a collection-level lens for reading Proverbs 28’s ruler and poverty sayings without treating every proverb as a report about one historical administration.

## Ecclesiastes 10:1 — “flies of death” and translation

- **Existing CKL material:** The chapter had no eligible passage-specific material.
- **Research question:** What translation and textual uncertainties lie behind “dead flies” in Ecclesiastes 10:1?
- **Verified source:** R. N. Whybray, *Ecclesiastes: The New Century Bible Commentary* (Eerdmans; Marshall, Morgan & Scott, 1989), p. 150. The complete scanned commentary and the verse note were inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Whybray notes that the Hebrew expression is literally “flies of death,” that a plural subject is followed by a singular verb, and that another singular verb and a phrase may represent glosses.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He lists dead, poisonous/lethal, and dying flies as possible senses, considers textual corruption likely, and still judges the general sense fairly clear. The CKL item records his analysis without choosing an emendation or claiming to resolve the Hebrew.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** This is one inspected commentary; the item does not collate ancient witnesses or survey every modern translation proposal.
- **Record:** `ecclesiastes-10-1-flies-of-death-translation`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers see why translations and textual notes diverge at the opening image and why the proverb’s point need not depend on a single reconstructed word.
