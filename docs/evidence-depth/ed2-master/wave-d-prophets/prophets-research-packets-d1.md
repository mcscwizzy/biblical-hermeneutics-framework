## Ezekiel 22:25 — “prophets” or “princes”?

- **Existing CKL material:** The ED-1A triage found no eligible passage-specific evidence in Ezekiel 22. The book’s existing context does not resolve the wording at 22:25.
- **Research question:** Why do English translations differ over whether Ezekiel 22:25 names a conspiracy of prophets or princes, and what textual basis supports the alternatives?
- **Verified source:** Sampson S. Ndoga, “Contemporary Reflections on Ezekiel 22:23–31 as a Depiction of Collective Responsibility of Leaders for National Demise,” *Old Testament Essays* 27, no. 1 (2014), 247–262, especially pp. 250–251. The full SciELO-hosted article was inspected.
- **Original-context evidence:** The verse belongs to the third of three judgment oracles in Ezekiel 22 and begins a sequence naming leaders, priests, officials, prophets, and the people. Verse 28 separately condemns prophets.
- **Textual observation:** Ndoga reports the Masoretic wording in 22:25 as “prophets,” while noting that several English translations use “princes.” He discusses the Septuagint rendering and describes “princes” as a proposed Hebrew emendation.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Ndoga considers “princes” contextually suitable for the predatory acts, while the “prophets” reading fits the Hebrew wording and must be assessed alongside the separate mention of prophets in v. 28. The record preserves the interpretive dispute instead of selecting a reading.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** The full article was consulted, but it is not a manuscript census or a full critical edition comparison. The item does not say that an extant Hebrew manuscript reads “princes,” and it does not claim to establish the original wording.
- **Record:** `ezekiel-22-prophets-princes-textual-reading`.
- **Reader relevance:** Explains why the English text may reflect an emendation and gives readers a concrete text-critical question to investigate.

## Ezekiel 31:1–18 — Pharaoh as a cedar descending to Sheol

- **Existing CKL material:** The ED-1A triage found no eligible passage-specific evidence in Ezekiel 31.
- **Research question:** How does the oracle’s cedar image connect imperial height, death, and the underworld, and how secure is a proposed Egyptian cultural resonance?
- **Verified source:** Lydia Lee, *Mapping Judah’s Fate in Ezekiel’s Oracles against the Nations* (Atlanta: SBL Press and Centro de Estudios de Historia del Antiguo Oriente, 2016), “The Chthonic Cedar,” pp. 139–144. The full open-access monograph was inspected through the UCA repository; the repository record lists a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
- **Original-context evidence:** Ezekiel 31:1 gives a relative exilic date. The oracle compares Pharaoh with Assyria and a great cedar nourished by deep waters; it moves from the cedar’s height and sheltering branches to its cutting down and descent to Sheol.
- **Textual observation:** Lee describes the passage’s literary movement through the cedar’s rise, judgment, and underworld fate, and notes a textual debate at v. 3 over reading “Assyria” or emending the word to “cypress.” This D1 record does not resolve that separate textual question.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Lee calls the image a “chthonic cedar” and argues that Egyptian associations among trees, Osiris, and afterlife traditions may illuminate Pharaoh’s underworld fate. She also reads the image among biblical and ancient Near Eastern royal and cosmic-tree traditions.
- **Uncertainty:** Lee presents Egyptian influence as possible; Ezekiel 31 does not name Osiris, and cultural resemblance does not prove direct borrowing or a particular ritual setting. The passage’s compositional history is not dated here.
- **Record:** `ezekiel-31-pharaoh-cedar-and-sheol`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects the political metaphor to mortality and afterlife imagery while showing where a cross-cultural comparison remains a hypothesis.

## Isaiah 10:5–8 — Assyria’s commission and its own imperial aims

- **Existing CKL material:** The ED-1A triage finds passage-specific material in Isaiah 10, including broad claims `isaiah-assyria-trust` and `isaiah-nations-empire`; it has no typed evidence anchored to 10:5–8. D1 adds a focused treatment of this local rhetorical contrast rather than duplicating a chapter summary.
- **Research question:** How does the oracle distinguish Assyria as an instrument of YHWH from the Assyrian king’s own ambition?
- **Verified source:** Shawn Zelig Aster, *Reflections of Empire in Isaiah 1–39: Responses to Assyrian Ideology* (Atlanta: SBL Press and Centro de Estudios de Historia del Antiguo Oriente, 2017), “Isaiah 10:5–15 as a Literary Unit,” pp. 184–190, especially p. 184. The full open repository edition was inspected through UCA.
- **Original-context evidence:** Isaiah 10:5–6 calls Assyria a rod and staff in YHWH’s hand and commissions it to plunder; vv. 7–8 then state that Assyria intends to destroy many nations and boasts of its rulers.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Aster reads the sequence as a limit on Assyrian imperial agency: the commission to despoil does not authorize the king’s stated intention to erase nations. He relates the rhetoric to Assyrian royal ideology and distinguishes tribute from vassal states from provincial taxation.
- **Uncertainty:** This is Aster’s literary-historical interpretation, not independent confirmation of the oracle’s theology. Aster proposes a Sargon II-era horizon for the unit; composition and redaction dates remain scholarly reconstructions and are not asserted as settled facts here.
- **Record:** `isaiah10-5-8-assyrian-agency-and-polemical-contrast`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers distinguish the text’s theological framing of empire from the empire’s own aims and political claims.
