# Wave E research packet — E1

E1 adds three passage-specific records in Luke, Acts, and Revelation. All remain draft/in-review and require human scholarly review. The batch creates no NE-9C or NE-10 relationship and introduces no reader-interface changes.

## Luke 5:30 — the verb for “grumbling”

- **Existing CKL material:** ED-1A identified Luke 5 as having no eligible passage-specific CKL material. The Luke object has broad Gospel context but no typed evidence item for this meal scene.
- **Research question:** Could the narrator’s word for the Pharisees’ objection evoke Israel’s wilderness complaints in Greek Scripture?
- **Verified sources:** Nicholas James Tuohy, *While They Were Eating: Lukan Mission through Domestic Hospitality and Ministry as Table-Service, and Implications for the Contemporary Church* (MPhil thesis, Australian Catholic University, 2012), pp. 93–94. The full thesis and these pages were inspected in ACU’s open repository. The SBL Greek New Testament was used for Luke’s Greek wording.
- **Original-context evidence:** Luke places the objection during Levi’s meal, between the call of Levi and a dispute about fasting. The narrator describes the Pharisees and scribes as grumbling about Jesus’ meal companions.
- **Textual observation:** Luke 5:30 uses ἐγόγγυζον. Tuohy notes related Septuagint usage for Israel’s wilderness complaints, including Exodus 15–16 and Numbers 14.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Tuohy proposes that the vocabulary may cast the critics’ objection as a form of wilderness-like grumbling. The record treats this as a possible resonance only: the verse has no quotation marker, and shared vocabulary does not prove deliberate allusion.
- **Major uncertainty/access limit:** The supporting discussion is in an MPhil thesis, not a peer-reviewed journal article. Its lexical observation is clear; the literary echo remains an interpretation with low confidence.
- **Record:** `luke-5-30-grumbling-and-wilderness-language`.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a specific Greek and scriptural-language feature to notice without claiming that Luke explicitly quotes Exodus or that the Pharisees represent all Jewish practice.

## Acts 15:29 — εὖ πράξετε

- **Existing CKL material:** Acts already includes claims on the decree’s textual variants, legal rationale, gentile inclusion, and the disputed relation to Galatians. It had no typed evidence item focused on the final phrase at 15:29.
- **Research question:** Does the letter’s closing εὖ πράξετε mean “you will fare well” or “you will do what is right,” and does the Greek evidence weigh the options equally?
- **Verified source:** John R. L. Moxon, “‘You Will Do Well’: But How, Exactly? A Curious Ending to the Apostolic Letter of Acts 15,” *Religions* 15, no. 8 (2024), article 947, DOI 10.3390/rel15080947, especially §§ 1.1, 2.2–2.4, 3, and 4. The full open-access article was inspected.
- **Original-context evidence:** Acts 15:28–29 presents the council’s letter and four abstentions; 15:29 ends with the unusual phrase εὖ πράξετε.
- **Textual observation:** Moxon surveys Greek usage and finds that “faring well/prospering” is more frequent in the selected samples, while a “commendable/correct action” sense is attested but less frequent. His article’s note 133 describes a selected sample of 549 instances, of which 62 referred unequivocally to right or noble action.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Frequency informs but does not settle the local reading. Moxon tests lexical possibilities against the letter’s rhetorical and compositional setting and leaves the phrase’s intended force open; he does not claim to resolve the decree’s purpose.
- **Major uncertainty:** The phrase is genuinely ambiguous in context. The Greek frequency sample is Moxon’s research method and selection, not an exhaustive census of every possible Lukan audience association.
- **Record:** `acts-15-29-eu-prassete-range`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows why the English closing line can carry different force and that lexical alternatives do not have identical distribution in the corpora studied.

## Revelation 13:11–18 — the land beast and Roman Asian institutions

- **Existing CKL material:** Revelation already has broad claims on the beasts, imperial context, economy, and the 666/616 textual variant, with source `friesen` identifying Friesen’s separate 2001 monograph. It had no typed passage-specific item for the social setting proposed for the land beast.
- **Research question:** Does the land beast point to one identifiable official, or can its imagery evoke a wider set of civic and cult institutions in Roman Asia?
- **Verified source:** Steven J. Friesen, “The Beast from the Land: Revelation 13:11–18 and Social Setting,” in David L. Barr, ed., *Reading the Book of Revelation: A Resource for Students* (Atlanta: Society of Biblical Literature, 2003), 49–64, especially pp. 60–64. The full SBL-hosted chapter and its cited argument were inspected. The separate 2003 chapter is not conflated with Friesen’s already-listed 2001 monograph.
- **Original-context evidence:** The vision depicts delegated authority, worship, an image, coercion, and economic exclusion, but does not name a historical office, priesthood, city, or social class.
- **Textual and historical evidence:** Friesen accepts a broad imperial-cult relation and uses inscriptions from Roman Asia to reconstruct provincial and local institutions and the careers of elite families. His discussion notes the *koinon*, provincial cult priesthoods, local cult offices, municipal representatives, and public events.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Friesen argues that the land-beast image evokes elite families operating through an interconnected civic and cultic network rather than encoding one specific institution or person. He reviews narrower proposals and says inscriptions aid interpretation without mechanically determining a symbol’s referent.
- **Major uncertainty:** The beast’s precise referent remains debated. Friesen’s inscription sample covers approximately 80–130 CE to contextualize the era in which Revelation is often placed; that evidence window is not a date for the book. The inscriptions document institutions and participation, not a single-to-one decoding of all visionary elements or uniform empire-wide enforcement.
- **Record:** `revelation-13-land-beast-as-asian-elite-network`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes a specific social-historical proposal available alongside the broad imperial context already in CKL, while preserving competing identifications and the limits of epigraphic inference.
