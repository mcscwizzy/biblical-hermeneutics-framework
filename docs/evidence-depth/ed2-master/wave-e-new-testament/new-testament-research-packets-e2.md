## Luke 8:1–3 — who is included in the healing and service clauses?

- **Existing CKL material:** The ED-1A census found no eligible passage-specific item for Luke 8. The Luke object has broad context about women in Luke–Acts but no typed evidence on the syntax of this list.
- **Research question:** Does Luke’s phrase “and many others” distinguish the additional women from the three named women, and does the following clause about providing for Jesus’ group apply to all of them?
- **Verified source:** Piotr Blajer, “The Women Who Followed Jesus: Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna in the Mission of Jesus (Luke 8:1–3),” *The Person and the Challenges* 16, no. 1 (2026), 7–21, especially pp. 10–11, DOI 10.15633/pch.16101. The full 15-page open-access article was inspected. Its journal page records the author, issue, DOI, publication date, and CC BY 4.0 license. The SBL Greek New Testament supports the passage’s Greek wording.
- **Original-context evidence:** Luke places the list in a summary of Jesus’ Galilean travel and proclamation, with the Twelve and several women accompanying the group. The passage names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna.
- **Textual observation:** Luke’s sequence moves from women described as healed to καὶ ἕτεραι πολλαί (“and many others”), followed by αἵτινες διηκόνουν αὐτοῖς (“who provided/ministered for them”). The antecedent of the relative clause is debated.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Blajer argues that ἕτεραι likely marks an additional group beyond the three named women, so the preceding healing description need not apply to everyone. He reports disagreement about whether the service clause refers to all the women or only the unnamed group and concludes that the Greek syntax does not settle it. The CKL item does not infer that every woman had been healed, that every woman was wealthy, or that service necessarily meant money.
- **Major uncertainty/access limit:** Blajer offers a plausible reading of ἕτεραι, not a syntactically compulsory result. The article also discusses a textual variant in the pronoun at the end of v. 3; no variant claim was added because primary apparatus and witness evidence were not independently reviewed for this batch.
- **Record:** `luke-8-1-3-women-scope`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers see how clause boundaries affect assumptions about women’s healing, participation, and material contribution without making unsupported claims about all first-century women.

## Luke 13:10–17 — necessity, permission, and Sabbath healing

- **Existing CKL material:** The ED-1A census found no eligible passage-specific item for Luke 13. The book has broad Sabbath and healing context but no typed evidence on the wording of the synagogue leader’s objection and Jesus’ response.
- **Research question:** Does the exchange frame the healing only as a question of what is permitted on the Sabbath, or does its repeated language of necessity make the woman’s release an obligation?
- **Verified source:** John Kilgallen, “The Obligation to Heal (Luke 13,10–17),” *Biblica* 82 (2001), 402–409, especially pp. 403–406 and 409. The complete article pages were inspected in the *Biblica* archive. The SBL Greek New Testament supports the Greek wording.
- **Original-context evidence:** Jesus teaches in a synagogue on the Sabbath and heals a woman described as bent over for eighteen years. The leader addresses the crowd about coming for cures on the six workdays; Jesus answers by speaking of the woman’s release and citing routine care for animals on the Sabbath.
- **Textual observation:** The leader uses δεῖ (“it is necessary”) in v. 14, and Jesus’ reply uses the same verb root in v. 16. The following animal example concerns routinely leading an ox or donkey to water, not an animal’s emergency rescue from a pit.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Kilgallen argues that the passage’s repeated necessity language makes the woman’s release central, rather than reducing the dispute to licitness alone. He reads the animal-care example as a lesser-to-greater argument about regular care. This is Kilgallen’s interpretation of Luke’s rhetoric, not a universal description of Jewish Sabbath law.
- **Major uncertainty/access limit:** The article is a single scholarly argument and does not survey the diversity of first-century Jewish Sabbath practice. Its discussion of Sabbath liberation imagery does not establish the historical origin of the healing story or a general legal rule.
- **Record:** `luke-13-10-17-sabbath-healing-necessity`.
- **Reader relevance:** Clarifies how a small Greek word and the narrative’s animal-care analogy shape the dispute while keeping Luke’s literary argument distinct from generalized claims about Judaism.
