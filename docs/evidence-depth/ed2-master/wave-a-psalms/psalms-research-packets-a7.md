# Wave A research packets — A7

This batch researches six baseline no-eligible chapters. Each item is passage-specific; the Psalm 61 and 63 royal-voice proposals are explicitly limited to an accessible publisher abstract. No claims, CKL objects, or authored cross-passage relationships were added.

## Psalm 52

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 52 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How does the poem connect wealth and destructive speech with its contrast between the wicked and the trusting worshipper?
- **Original-context evidence:** The body of the psalm contrasts a powerful figure who boasts in evil and trusts riches with a speaker who trusts God's steadfast love and is compared to a green olive tree.
- **Verified source and locator:** Philippus J. Botha, “‘I am like a green olive tree’: The Wisdom context of Psalm 52,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 69, no. 1 (2013), article a1962, sections on Proverbs 1–3 and Jeremiah 9. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v69i1.1962) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The poem's speech and wealth critique is explicit; its final image contrasts the antagonist's uprooting with the speaker's rootedness and trust.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha proposes a wisdom-shaped allusion network involving Proverbs, Psalm 49, and Jeremiah 9, and a late Persian-period social context. He reports Hossfeld and Zenger's view that the wisdom connections are less direct and an earlier form is possible.
- **Major uncertainty:** Direct literary dependence, the community's social setting, the date, and the relation of the superscription to composition remain unsettled. Botha counts the superscription as Hebrew verse 1; CKL references use BHF body numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-52-wealth-critique-and-wisdom-intertexts` (Psalm 52:1–9).
- **Reader relevance:** The wisdom reading helps readers connect exploitative speech and wealth with the poem's image of trust and flourishing without turning the Doeg heading into a composition date.

## Psalm 59

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 59 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How do the poem's evening, night, and morning references shape its movement from danger to confidence?
- **Original-context evidence:** The enemies return in the evening and may remain through the night; the speaker vows to sing of God's strength and goodness in the morning.
- **Verified source and locator:** Bernd Janowski, *Arguing with God: A Theological Anthropology of the Psalms*, trans. Armin Siedlecki (Louisville, KY: Westminster John Knox Press, 2013), pp. 100–104 of the Psalm 59 analysis. [Full selection](https://discere.pusc.it/pluginfile.php/131068/mod_folder/content/0/7.2.%20Salmo%2059_Janowski%2C%20Arguing%20with%20God%2C%202014%2C%20pp.%20100-114.pdf?forcedownload=1) inspected; book identity and publication year were checked against bibliographic records.
- **Textual observation:** Repeated evening descriptions and trust language frame two movements; morning praise follows the threat imagery.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Janowski reads the temporal pattern as a literary design that carries complaint toward trust. He notes that scholars disagree whether a repeated unit functions as a refrain or reflects textual variation.
- **Major uncertainty:** This pattern does not establish a historical siege. Janowski's paired references include Hebrew numbering that counts the superscription; the item uses BHF body-verse numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-59-evening-threat-and-morning-confidence` (Psalm 59:1–17).
- **Reader relevance:** The temporal sequence helps readers track the prayer's rhetorical change while keeping the heading's Saul setting distinct from independent historical evidence.

## Psalm 61

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 61 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** Does the combination of shelter-under-wings language and the prayer for the king support reading the first-person voice as royal?
- **Original-context evidence:** The petitioner asks to dwell in God's tent and shelter beneath God's wings, then asks that the king's days be prolonged. The text does not directly identify the first-person speaker as the king.
- **Verified source and locator:** Joel M. LeMon, “The ‘Hezekiah Seal,’ Judahite Iconography, and the Winged Form of God in Psalms 61 and 63,” *Hebrew Bible and Ancient Israel* 13, no. 1 (2024), 33–62, publisher abstract. [Publisher record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1628/hebai-2024-0004) inspected; full article PDF was unavailable.
- **Textual observation:** Protective and royal language occurs in one prayer but does not name a historical speaker or king.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** LeMon's abstract says that analysis of a bulla strengthens a royal-voice reading of Psalms 61 and 63. The abstract does not expose the argument's steps.
- **Major uncertainty:** The full article, bulla, iconographic argument, and archaeological context were not inspected. No claim about the object's attribution, date, or provenience is made.
- **New record:** `psalm-61-royal-voice-proposal` (Psalm 61:4–7), low confidence due to abstract-only access.
- **Reader relevance:** This gives readers a source-identified royal-voice proposal and a clear reason to treat it as provisional.

## Psalm 63

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 63 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How might the poem's shelter-under-wings image relate to its closing reference to the king?
- **Original-context evidence:** The speaker takes shelter under God's wings and the poem ends by saying the king will rejoice in God; it does not name the first-person speaker.
- **Verified source and locator:** The same LeMon article abstract cited for Psalm 61. [Publisher record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1628/hebai-2024-0004) only; full article PDF unavailable.
- **Textual observation:** The wing image and royal statement are both part of the poem's final movement.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** LeMon's abstract reports that a bulla's iconography strengthens a royal-voice interpretation of Psalms 61 and 63; this CKL item records that proposal without adopting it as settled.
- **Major uncertainty:** The source's abstract was the only article text accessible. The article's supporting argument and artifact details remain unreviewed.
- **New record:** `psalm-63-royal-voice-proposal` (Psalm 63:7–11), low confidence due to abstract-only access.
- **Reader relevance:** Readers can notice the text's royal language and explore the proposal without confusing a modern interpretation with an identified ancient king.

## Psalm 64

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 64 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How does Psalm 64 turn the enemies' verbal attack into a sequence of divine reversal and public response?
- **Original-context evidence:** The poem moves from petition and characterization of the attackers through God's counterattack to the response of observers and the righteous.
- **Verified source and locator:** Sigrid Eder, “Storytelling in the Psalter? Chances and Limits of a Narrative Psalm Analysis – Shown Exemplarily in Psalm 64,” *Old Testament Essays* 32, no. 2 (2019), 343–357, PDF pp. 347–348 and 354–355. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2019/v32n2a5) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Cruel words are compared to weapons; the attackers stumble over their own tongues; the poem closes with fear, understanding, refuge, and rejoicing.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Eder reads the poem as two main movements, with forward-driving parallelism building tension. She cautions that narratology supplements rather than replaces analysis of metaphor and semantics.
- **Major uncertainty:** The reading describes the poem's literary sequence, not a recoverable historical event. Eder's verse numbers count the superscription; the item uses BHF body numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-64-attack-to-vindication-narrative-movement` (Psalm 64:1–10).
- **Reader relevance:** The narrative lens clarifies how poetic images work together and keeps the text's movement visible without recasting lyric as prose history.

## Psalm 65

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census; no exact Psalm 65 evidence anchor was found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How do Zion and harvest imagery fit together with Psalm 65's references to distant peoples and the wider world?
- **Original-context evidence:** The poem moves from Zion and the temple to the earth's farthest reaches, then describes water, grain, rain, pastures, hills, meadows, and valleys joining in praise.
- **Verified source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “‘Ascribe Power to God, Whose Majesty Is over Israel, and Whose Power Is in the Skies’ (Psalm 68:35): The Theological Intent of Psalms 65–68 and Their Connections with the Prologue to the Psalter (Psalms 1–2),” *Old Testament Essays* 36, no. 1 (2023), 66–88, especially 69–74, 79–80, 86–88. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2023/v36n1a5) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Sanctuary, distant reaches, rain, crops, and creation's praise appear in a single poem.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha proposes that Psalms 65–68 form an editorially arranged cluster and argues that the rain passage's erets is better rendered “earth” than “land” in that context. The cluster reading is not a historical report or proof of a harvest rite.
- **Major uncertainty:** Editorial grouping and the translation choice are interpretive proposals. The source numbers Hebrew verses with the superscription; the CKL anchor uses BHF body numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-65-zion-to-earth-praise-sequence` (Psalm 65:1–13).
- **Reader relevance:** The poem's agricultural imagery can be read as both concrete provision and part of a broader vision of creation and universal praise.
