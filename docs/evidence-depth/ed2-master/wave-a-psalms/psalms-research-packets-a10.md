## Psalm 53 — differences from Psalm 14 and the surrounding collection

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record for Psalm 53. The reused Botha source identity already supports a separate Psalm 14 record.
- **Research question:** Should Psalm 53’s differences from Psalm 14 be treated as corruption, or as deliberate changes for its position among Psalms 52–55?
- **Source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “Psalm 53 in canonical perspective,” *Old Testament Essays* 26, no. 3 (2013), 583–606; full article, especially the discussion of Psalm 53’s structure and the comparison with Psalm 14. [Full open-access text](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v26n3/04.pdf) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Botha analyzes Psalm 53 as a six-line poem and identifies distinct wording and a different ending from Psalm 14. His article numbers the superscription as verse 1; its Psalm 53:2–7 corresponds to BHF Psalm 53:1–6.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha argues that Psalm 53 intentionally adapts Psalm 14 to work within the Psalms 52–55 cluster. He connects the cluster’s biographical superscriptions with a David–Saul reading as a canonical frame, while acknowledging the reverse proposal that Psalm 14 developed from Psalm 53.
- **Uncertainty:** The direction of dependence, redactional setting, and cluster history are scholarly reconstructions. The superscriptions do not establish Davidic authorship or a historical occasion.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-53-adaptation-and-cluster-context`; existing source ID `botha-psalm53-canonical-2013` reused.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a reason to compare the two poems’ differences without treating one as a defective copy or converting a canonical reading into biography.

## Psalm 102 — “created” or “set apart” in the Zion stanza

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific evidence for Psalm 102.
- **Research question:** Does the clause about the people who praise Yah describe a future-created population or the present status of Zion’s people?
- **Source and locator:** Ellen van Wolde, “Psalm 102.13–23: Qualifications Rather Than Actions,” *The Bible Translator* 70, no. 2 (2019), 207–222, especially the analysis of Psalm 102:19b and its proposed translation, printed pp. 217–219. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.1177/2051677019851843) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The Hebrew clause in the source’s Psalm 102:19b is BHF Psalm 102:18b. Many translations take ברא as “create” and render a future “newly created people”; van Wolde parses the phrase as a description rather than an action.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Van Wolde argues that the syntax and the participle’s resultative force favor “a people set apart—it praises Yah,” relating the people to Zion’s inhabitants and the surrounding nations.
- **Uncertainty:** “Create” is the standard interpretation and the meaning of ברא is contested. Van Wolde’s proposal is a full-text, attributed translation argument, not a settled lexical consensus. She counts the superscription as verse 1, so the source’s 13–23 unit maps to BHF 12–22.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-102-created-or-set-apart-people`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how syntax and verse numbering can change whether the stanza is read as a future prediction or as a description of Zion’s community.

## Psalm 109 — whose speech is the central imprecation?

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific evidence for Psalm 109.
- **Research question:** Is the psalmist speaking the severe words in the central imprecation, or reporting words spoken by enemies?
- **Source and locator:** Stephen Egwim, “Determining the Place of vv. 6–19 in Ps 109: A Case Presentation Analysis,” *Ephemerides Theologicae Lovanienses* 80, no. 1 (April 2004), 112–130, publisher abstract and bibliographic record. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.2143/ETL.80.1.504559) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The accessible abstract identifies both speaker assignments and says there is no generally accepted position. It describes existing arguments as relying primarily on linguistic analysis.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Egwim’s abstract proposes a legal case-presentation setting and argues that hearing the imprecation as reported speech makes the poem cohere as a lament and prayer.
- **Uncertainty and access limit:** The full text was not accessible. The record reports only the abstract’s conclusion and cannot evaluate the linguistic or legal argument. The abstract does not state whether its verses count the superscription; BHF’s exact chapter anchor avoids claiming a conversion for vv. 6–19. Low confidence and full-text/human review are required.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-109-speaker-and-reported-speech-debate`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the discourse question visible while showing that the proposed reported-speech reading remains one argument within an unresolved scholarly dispute.

## Psalm 135 — servants, firstborn, and Exodus memory

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific evidence for Psalm 135.
- **Research question:** Why does the hymn contrast YHWH’s temple servants with Pharaoh’s servants and emphasize the firstborn of both people and animals?
- **Source and locator:** Dirk Human, “Psalms 135 and 136: Exodus Motifs Contributing to Israelite Praise,” *Old Testament Essays* 34, no. 2 (2021), 646–668, Psalm 135 section C.4.c, printed pp. 656–657; structure and proposed setting, pp. 650–653. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2021/v34n2a19) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 135:1 summons YHWH’s servants in the temple; verses 8–9 recall the firstborn plague and signs against Pharaoh and his servants. The poem places these deeds after a creation section and before its contrast with powerless idols.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Human reads the human-and-animal firstborn as a merism emphasizing the plague tradition’s scope and notes its relationship to Exodus 12:12, 29. He reads “servants” in verses 1 and 9 contrastively: YHWH’s servants praise, while Pharaoh’s servants experience judgment.
- **Uncertainty:** This is an interpretation of the psalm’s use of Exodus tradition, not independent corroboration of the narrated events. Human treats Second Temple worship and a post-exilic horizon as plausible; neither a specific festival nor a composition date is established.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-135-exodus-motifs-and-servants`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects a small repeated word and a striking detail to the hymn’s movement from creation through remembered deliverance to praise.
