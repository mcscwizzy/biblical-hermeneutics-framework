# Wave A research packets — A9

A9 reviews six Psalm chapters that had no eligible passage-specific material in the baseline census. Four additions met the source and relevance standard; Psalms 58 and 76 remain deferred because the accessible material did not support a sufficiently verified, passage-specific claim. The four new records remain draft/in-review.

## Psalm 57 — praise reused in Psalm 108

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census.
- **Research question:** How does the confidence and praise section function differently when reused in Psalm 108?
- **Source and locator:** Philippus J. Botha, “Psalm 108 and the quest for closure,” *Old Testament Essays* 23/3 (2010), 574–596, section D, especially printed pp. 585–586. [Full open-access article](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v23n3/05.pdf) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 57 moves from urgent petition through confidence to praise and an appeal. Psalm 108 reuses its praise passage with changes in wording and placement.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha argues that Psalm 108 replaces Psalm 60’s lament with the praise section from Psalm 57. There, confidence follows an individual plea; in Psalm 108, its new position gives it the character of an individual praise song.
- **Uncertainty:** The literary reuse is Botha’s reading, not proof of a historical battle or Psalm 57’s composition date. His article numbers superscriptions as verses: BHF Psalm 57:7–11 corresponds to its Psalm 57:8–12.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-57-reuse-in-psalm108-reframes-praise`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how a passage can acquire meaning from a new literary setting while keeping the donor text’s own movement in view.

## Psalm 69 — restoration, servants, and the closing stanza

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census.
- **Research question:** How does the closing move from individual suffering to cosmic praise and Zion’s restoration?
- **Source and locator:** Michael A. Lyons, “The servants in Psalms 22, 69, and 102,” in M. A. Lyons and Jacob Stromberg (eds.), *Isaiah’s Servants in Early Judaism and Christianity*, WUNT II 554 (Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 2021), 45–64, section 2.2, discussion of Psalm 69:35–37 (Lyons counts the superscription as verse 1; BHF’s corresponding anchor is Psalm 69:34–36) and Isaiah 65:9. [Full peer-reviewed accepted manuscript in the University of St Andrews repository](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/28157) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Verses 35–37 widen the poem to praise by heaven, earth, and sea, then describe Zion’s rescue and rebuilding and the inheritance of the servants’ offspring.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Lyons argues that the shift in scope and shared language with Isaiah 40–66, especially Isaiah 65:9, supports treating these verses as a later addition that coordinates the psalm with Isaiah.
- **Uncertainty:** The source summarizes other compositional models, and the relation between the ending and the lament remains disputed. The addition model is attributed; it does not establish a direct borrowing route or a date for the entire psalm.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-69-restoration-stanza-and-servants`; corrected to BHF’s canonical verse numbering after the all-chapter audit flagged the source’s superscription-counting convention.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers understand why the ending’s communal restoration horizon may broaden the individual lament.

## Psalm 94 — weekday-song reception and literary sequence

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census.
- **Research question:** What does Psalm 94’s place in Jewish weekday worship reveal about later reception, and what does it not establish about original composition?
- **Source and locator:** Yaakov Jaffe, “Why Is Psalm 94 the Song of the Day for Wednesday?,” *Jewish Bible Quarterly* 54/2 (April–June 2026), 122–132; sections on form, placement, content, and the splitting of the sea. [Full article HTML](https://www.jewishbible.org/articles/why-is-psalm-94-song-of-the-day-for-wednesday/) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 94 is an extended poetic appeal for justice with parallel lines and unusual diction; in the Psalter it follows Psalm 93. Jaffe describes its later assignment as the weekday song for Wednesday.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Jaffe argues that the poem’s form, language, and placement support reading Psalm 94 as the second half of Psalm 93 and proposes that a shared sea-crossing theme explains the weekday assignment.
- **Uncertainty:** This is a modern scholarly account of a later Jewish liturgical tradition and a literary proposal. It does not prove that Psalms 93–94 originated as one composition or identify Psalm 94’s original performance setting.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-94-weekday-song-and-poetic-sequence`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes later Jewish reception visible without projecting it backward as the psalm’s original setting.

## Psalm 99 — Zion in the YHWH-kingship sequence

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the baseline census.
- **Research question:** Does Psalm 99’s unique reference to Zion establish when the psalm or its Book IV placement arose?
- **Source and locator:** Mark W. Hamilton, “Narrating YHWH’s Kingship in Psalms,” *Kairos: Evangelical Journal of Theology* 17/2 (2023), 135–147, section discussing Psalm 99:2 and its placement among Psalms 93–100. The existing source identity `hamilton-yhwh-kingship-psalms-2023` was reused; full open-access article inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 99 proclaims YHWH’s kingship and holiness, locates YHWH at Zion in verse 2, and recalls Moses, Aaron, and Samuel.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Hamilton reports a proposal that the singular Zion reference entered the kingship sequence at a relatively late stage. He offers a final-form reading in which Zion is the worshiping community’s location and argues that this placement alone cannot date the poem or its incorporation.
- **Uncertainty:** The compositional significance of the unique Zion reference remains disputed. Hamilton’s counter-reading is an interpretation; the source does not establish a composition date.
- **CKL record:** `psalm-99-zion-and-kingship-sequence`.
- **Reader relevance:** Separates an observable place-name from the chronology some readers might infer from it.
