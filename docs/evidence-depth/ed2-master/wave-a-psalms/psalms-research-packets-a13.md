## Psalm 34 — teacher-student address and wisdom rhetoric

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage, with no passage-anchored claims, typed evidence, or passage-specific interpretive notes.
- **Research question:** Does Psalm 34’s instructional address justify calling the whole poem a “wisdom psalm”?
- **Verified source:** William P. Brown, “‘Come, O Children … I Will Teach You the Fear of the Lord’ (Psalm 34:12): Comparing Psalms and Proverbs,” in *Seeking Out the Wisdom of the Ancients*, pp. 85–102 (2005), DOI 10.5325/j.ctv1w36pmb.11. The JSTOR bibliographic record and excerpt were inspected.
- **Textual observation:** In BHF numbering, Psalm 34:11–14 shifts to a teacher addressing “children” and teaching the fear of YHWH through instructions about speech, peace, and life.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Brown argues that comparison with Proverbs can identify shared rhetoric without classifying the entire Psalm as a wisdom psalm. His visible framing itself cautions that the genre criteria are unsettled.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** The full chapter was inaccessible. The record is limited to the publisher excerpt and explicitly discloses this.
- **Record:** `psalm-34-instruction-and-wisdom-rhetoric`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers notice the instructional voice without assuming an ancient school setting or a settled genre label.

## Psalm 40 — reception in 4Q160

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage, without passage-anchored material.
- **Research question:** How did a Second Temple Jewish composition reuse Psalm 40’s language of rescue and petition?
- **Verified source:** Alex P. Jassen, “Intertextual Readings of the Psalms in the Dead Sea Scrolls: 4Q160 (Samuel Apocryphon) and Psalm 40,” *Revue de Qumran* 22, no. 3 (2006), 403–430, DOI 10.2143/RQ.22.3.3290217. The publisher abstract and bibliographic record were inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Jassen identifies Psalm 40:2–4 as a source model for language and structure in 4Q160 3–5 ii 1–4.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He reads Psalm 40 as moving from remembered divine aid toward a present petition; he argues that 4Q160 combines those literary functions into one prayer.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** Only the abstract was available. The proposed relation is literary reuse, not evidence that 4Q160 is a biblical manuscript witness to Psalm 40’s wording.
- **Record:** `psalm-40-reception-in-4q160`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes later Jewish engagement with a Psalm visible while keeping reception history separate from the Psalm’s original composition.

## Psalm 73 — ring-composition proposal

- **Existing CKL material:** Entity-only coverage; no passage-specific claims, evidence, or interpretive notes.
- **Research question:** Does the Psalm’s closing declaration return to its opening claim through a central turning point?
- **Verified source:** Gregory T. K. Wong, “Psalm 73 as Ring Composition,” *Biblica* 97, no. 1 (2016), 16–40, DOI 10.2143/BIB.97.1.3183471. The publisher abstract and bibliographic record were inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The Psalm opens by affirming God’s goodness, turns through the speaker’s crisis and sanctuary experience, and closes with a declaration that nearness to God is good.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Wong proposes a ring composition in which the opening and ending merge around a turning point, with corresponding sections arranged chiastically.
- **Uncertainty/access limit:** The full article was inaccessible; the abstract does not provide the detailed structural markers. Wong’s reading is therefore low-confidence and not presented as consensus.
- **Record:** `psalm-73-ring-composition-proposal`.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a testable structural question for a Psalm whose speaker moves from envy to renewed trust.

## Psalm 78 — history as instruction

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage, without passage-specific claims, typed evidence, or interpretive notes.
- **Research question:** How do the Psalm’s historical recollections serve its teaching frame?
- **Verified source:** Yeol Kim and H. F. van Rooy, “Reading Psalm 78 multidimensionally: the textual dimension,” *Scriptura* 74 (2000), 285–298. The full repository PDF was inspected, especially pp. 285–287.
- **Textual observation:** Verses 1–8 address hearers and call the next generation to learn; verses 9–72 recount Israel’s past through repeated patterns of failure and divine response.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Kim and van Rooy divide the Psalm into an introduction (vv. 1–8) and two recitals (vv. 9–39 and 40–72) and describe it as mixed historical and didactic poetry.
- **Uncertainty:** The article states that scholars disagree substantially over structure, genre, date, and *Sitz im Leben*, with proposed settings ranging from the tenth century to the postexilic period. No composition date or performance context is asserted here.
- **Record:** `psalm-78-introduction-and-historical-didactic-recitations`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how the Psalm turns historical memory into instruction without treating the poem as a neutral chronicle.
