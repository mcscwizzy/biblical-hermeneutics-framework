# Wave A research packets — A4

A4 examines eight remaining no-eligible Psalms selected from the A3 chapter census. Each item is passage-specific and draft/in-review; eligibility does not establish adequate depth. No relation edge is authored.

## Psalm 46 — Roaring waters and the stream that gladdens the city

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How does the contrast between roaring waters and the city stream shape the poem's account of security?
- **Original-context evidence:** Psalm 46:2–5 pairs upheaval in creation with a river that gladdens God's city and God's presence at its center.
- **Verified source and locator:** Jo-Marí Schäder, “Understanding (the Lack of) Space in Psalm 47:6 in Light of its Neighbouring Psalms,” *Old Testament Essays* 23, no. 1 (2010), PDF pp. 143–146. Full open-access article inspected: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.10520/EJC86030).
- **Textual observation:** Water imagery moves from destabilization to an image associated with the city and divine presence.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Schäder's spatial reading places the city/temple at an ordered center and reads the adjacent poems as a sequence; this does not identify a literal river or a specific historical attack.
- **Major uncertainty:** Pagination is inconsistent: the online issue index lists 139–160; the university repository and PDF's internal page numbers list 129–160. The cited page locator follows the PDF's printed numbering. The poem's historical occasion remains unknown.
- **New record:** `psalm-46-chaos-waters-and-stream-imagery`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how spatial and water imagery advances the poem's argument without turning poetic space into a map.

## Psalm 56 — Davidic heading as a literary frame

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** What does the David-in-Gath superscription contribute to the prayer's fear and trust language?
- **Original-context evidence:** The heading names David and Gath; the body voices fear, trust, hostile speech, and praise. The heading is a textual cue, not independent evidence about David's life.
- **Verified source and locator:** J. H. (Henk) Potgieter, “Psalm 56 Read within its Literary Context in the Psalter and its Connections with King David,” *Old Testament Essays* 28, no. 3 (2015), 760–776, especially 760–763. Full article: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2015/v28n3a12).
- **Textual observation:** The superscription provides a narrative frame; the prayer repeatedly sets fear against trust.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Potgieter reads the heading as an editorial/metatextual connection to 1 Samuel 21 and proposes that it reorients the reader's hearing of the prayer.
- **Major uncertainty:** Potgieter notes that no biblical narrative explicitly says David was seized in Gath. The source numbers the superscription as v. 1 and the body as vv. 2–14; the CKL anchor uses the 13 body-verse numbering in BHF's Psalm text.
- **New record:** `psalm-56-superscription-and-editorial-frame`.
- **Reader relevance:** Distinguishes an interpretive heading from independently established biography or authorship.

## Psalm 77 — Lament, recollection, and praise

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How does the poem's shift to remembered divine action change the speaker's lament?
- **Original-context evidence:** The prayer moves from distress and questions to recollection of divine acts and a closing account of God's leading through waters.
- **Verified source and locator:** Liswanisu Kamuwanga, “Exile and Suffering: Reading Psalm 77 in African Context,” *Old Testament Essays* 20, no. 3 (2007), 720–735, especially 720–723. Full article: [University of Pretoria repository PDF](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstreams/d41787cf-9250-42f6-9f88-3cba2168cecd/download).
- **Textual observation:** Kamuwanga describes complaint, confidence, and praise, and observes that the prayer has no explicit petition.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He reads remembered divine action as a reorientation from present distress. The record uses this formal analysis, not his proposed exile setting or broader mythological reconstruction.
- **Major uncertainty:** A lament's form does not establish its original audience or date. The article was presented at a scholarly congress as part of the author's doctoral research; his contemporary African comparison is not used as evidence for ancient context.
- **New record:** `psalm-77-lament-memory-and-exodus-reframing`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes visible the poem's turn from questioning to remembered history while preserving uncertainty about its origin.

## Psalm 91 — Wisdom language and protective instruction

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How does the poem's protective imagery relate to Israelite wisdom instruction?
- **Original-context evidence:** The psalm combines refuge and shelter with a protected way, protection of the foot, fear language, and concluding divine assurance.
- **Verified source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “Psalm 91 and its Wisdom Connections,” *Old Testament Essays* 25, no. 2 (2012), 260–276, especially 260–264 and 268–270. Full article consulted: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.10520/EJC122064).
- **Textual observation:** Botha compares the psalm's way, foot, and fear motifs with Proverbs 3.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He argues that the psalm's present form participates in wisdom instruction and proposes that it influenced Job 5:17–26.
- **Major uncertainty:** Literary dependence, direction, and editorial setting are not independently established by shared motifs; no date or temple origin is adopted.
- **New record:** `psalm-91-wisdom-language-and-protection-instruction`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects protective language with wisdom teaching without treating the poem as a guarantee that all worshippers avoid harm.

## Psalm 92 — Central confession and ordered-world imagery

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How does the poem's central confession shape its answer to apparent disorder?
- **Original-context evidence:** Tucker identifies the short confession in the poem's center and relates its creation imagery to the repeated claim that YHWH is exalted.
- **Verified source and locator:** W. Dennis Tucker Jr., “The Ordered World of Psalm 92,” *Old Testament Essays* 32, no. 2 (2019), 358–377, especially 361–365. Full article: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2019/v32n2a6).
- **Textual observation:** Tucker counts seven couplets/triplets on either side of a central monocolon and treats the surrounding creation imagery as significant.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** He argues that the form reinforces a confession of order under YHWH. He cautions that proposed chiastic structures are forced.
- **Major uncertainty:** Tucker's exile-oriented setting is a historical reconstruction, not a secure date. His source numbers the superscription as v. 1; source v. 9 corresponds to BHF body v. 8.
- **New record:** `psalm-92-creation-order-and-sabbath-song`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how the poem's structure contributes to its theology without requiring a disputed chiasm.

## Psalm 105 — Historical recital as liturgical teaching

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How might a communal recital of ancestral and exodus traditions function in worship?
- **Original-context evidence:** Psalm 105 calls the audience to remember and praise while recounting divine actions from ancestral promises through the exodus and settlement traditions.
- **Verified source and locator:** Sue Gillingham, “Psalms 105 and 106 and the Participation in History through Liturgy,” *Hebrew Bible and Ancient Israel* 4, no. 4 (2015), 450–475. The [publisher page](https://doi.org/10.1628/219222715X14604539713420) gives bibliographic details and the abstract; the full article was not accessible. Publisher page dates online publication to 2018.
- **Textual observation:** The psalm's recital links communal praise with remembered acts and covenant promises.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Gillingham's abstract proposes Psalms 104–106 as a liturgical collection in which history could be learned through performance, including Second Temple worship.
- **Major uncertainty:** Only the abstract was consulted. The claim is kept at low confidence and does not treat Psalm 105 as independent verification of the events narrated.
- **New record:** `psalm-105-history-recounted-as-liturgy`.
- **Reader relevance:** Invites readers to consider how recited history could teach a worshipping community while distinguishing memory from independent historical corroboration.

## Psalm 111 — Alphabetic form and scribal learning

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** What can an alphabetic acrostic suggest about the poem's literary and learning environment?
- **Original-context evidence:** Psalm 111's Masoretic form follows the Hebrew alphabet while praising YHWH's works, righteousness, and covenant faithfulness.
- **Verified source and locator:** Gert T. M. Prinsloo, “Reading Psalm 112 as a ‘Midrash’ on Psalm 111,” *Old Testament Essays* 32, no. 2 (2019), 636–668, especially 636–640 and 659. Full article: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2019/v32n2a19).
- **Textual observation:** The poems are complete colon-based acrostics in the Masoretic tradition.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Prinsloo proposes that the graphic convention probably presumes a culture of writing and reading and may have developed in scribal and wisdom schools.
- **Major uncertainty:** A form-based social proposal does not identify a specific school, author, date, or original performance setting. The item adds no relationship edge to Psalm 112.
- **New record:** `psalm-111-acrostic-praise-and-scribal-learning`.
- **Reader relevance:** Links a visible poetic pattern to a cautiously framed question about writing and learning in ancient Israel.

## Psalm 139 — The imprecation and ambiguity in prayer

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific item in the A3 census.
- **Research question:** How do the imprecation in vv. 19–22 and the final request to be searched relate to the prayer?
- **Original-context evidence:** The poem shifts from divine knowledge and presence to an imprecation against the wicked, then closes with a request for God to search and lead the speaker.
- **Verified source and locator:** David G. Firth, “Psalm 139: A Study in Ambiguity,” *Old Testament Essays* 32, no. 2 (2019), 491–510, especially 491–493 and 507–508. Full article: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2019/v32n2a13).
- **Textual observation:** The late tonal shift and the relation of vv. 19–22 to the poem remain central interpretive questions.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Firth proposes a unified reading as a prayer of the accused, with ambiguity allowing innocent and guilty worshippers to hear the prayer differently.
- **Major uncertainty:** Firth's proposal is one reading within a disputed integrity debate; the record does not settle whether vv. 19–22 are original or secondary.
- **New record:** `psalm-139-imprecation-and-ambiguity-in-prayer`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers ask why the prayer's imprecation is followed by a request for divine searching without flattening the interpretive dispute.
