## Psalm 39 — illness-lament form and delayed retribution

- **Existing CKL material:** 0 eligible passage-specific items in the current backlog; broad Psalter context remains in the book object.
- **Research question:** Does the prayer's illness-lament form require a recoverable sick-person or cultic setting, or can it function as a wisdom reflection on mortality and delayed retribution?
- **Original-context evidence:** The speaker resolves to guard speech while the wicked are present, reflects on the brevity of human life, describes divine discipline, and asks for a brief respite. Psalm 39 is positioned at the end of the first Davidic collection (Psalms 3–41).
- **Verified source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “Psalm 39 and its Place in the Development of a Doctrine of Retribution in the Hebrew Bible,” *Old Testament Essays* 30, no. 2 (2017), 240–264, especially 241–244, 251–253, and 261–263. Full open-access article consulted through the [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2017/v30n2a4) and [University of Pretoria repository PDF](https://repository.up.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/d5138b17-0001-48f5-a51f-1c14a28051f1/content).
- **Textual observation:** The poem's first-person restraint, mortality reflection, and plea for relief coexist; its lament-like form does not by itself establish illness or a ritual use.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha argues that the poem's form serves a wisdom reflection on the frustration caused by delayed retribution, responding to the confident expectations in Psalms 34 and 37. He contrasts this with interpretations that take the illness language as a direct historical setting.
- **Major uncertainty:** The illness/cultic setting, relationship to neighboring psalms, redactional history, and composition date remain debated. Botha's proposed late post-exilic horizon is not adopted as a date. His article counts the superscription as v. 1; BHF's anchor follows the body-verse numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-39-wisdom-reflection-and-delayed-retribution`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the tension between the poem's lament form and its reflection on apparently delayed justice visible without treating one reconstruction as settled.

## Psalm 48 — inspected Zion and remembered space

- **Existing CKL material:** 0 eligible passage-specific items in the current backlog; broad Psalter context remains in the book object.
- **Research question:** How does the poem's invitation to inspect Jerusalem's towers and rampart function when its assurance is later remembered in the face of destruction?
- **Original-context evidence:** The poem recalls what the community has heard and seen, praises God's steadfast love and justice, directs hearers to walk around Zion and inspect its structures, and calls for testimony to the next generation.
- **Verified source and locator:** Till Magnus Steiner, “Perceived and Narrated Space in Psalm 48,” *Old Testament Essays* 25, no. 3 (2012), 685–704, especially 686, 692–694, and 700–701. Full open-access PDF consulted at [SciELO](https://scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v25n3/14.pdf); journal metadata and abstract are also available [here](https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S1010-99192012000300014&script=sci_abstract).
- **Textual observation:** The city survey and the poem's remembered testimony give physical structures a rhetorical role; they are not archaeological evidence that a particular attack occurred.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Steiner proposes an earlier layer that grounds Zion's security in perceived space and an exilic redaction that turns the city into remembered and narrated space after destruction. He also reports that proposed historical occasions span a wide chronology and that the poem names no one specific event.
- **Major uncertainty:** The proposed layers and exilic setting are a literary-historical model. The original audience of the city-survey imperatives is debated. Steiner follows BHS numbering, which counts the superscription; BHF uses body-verse numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-48-zion-security-and-remembered-space`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects the poem's visible city imagery with the role of collective memory while distinguishing literary geography from archaeological or event claims.

## Psalm 62 — personal trust and community instruction

- **Existing CKL material:** 0 eligible passage-specific items in the current backlog; broad Psalter context remains in the book object.
- **Research question:** How does the direct address to “people” and the warning about wealth relate to the speaker's personal trust and accusation?
- **Original-context evidence:** The speaker's repeated confession of refuge shifts to plural exhortations to trust and pour out the heart before God, followed by warnings against extortion, robbery, and setting one's heart on wealth.
- **Verified source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “Psalm 62: Prayer, Accusation, Declaration of Innocence, Self-Motivation, Sermon, or All of These?” *Acta Theologica* 38, no. 2 (2018), 32–48, especially 32–36 and 41–46. Full open-access article and PDF consulted at the [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18820/23099089/actat.v38i2.3) and [SciELO](https://scielo.org.za/pdf/at/v38n2/03.pdf).
- **Textual observation:** The move from first-person confession to communal imperatives and economic warnings is explicit in the poem. Botha reviews multiple proposals about the poem's form and use.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha reads the poem as a unitary, wisdom-shaped argument: the speaker's example supports a community exhortation not to rely on power or wealth. He proposes a post-exilic setting shaped by economic hardship and competition.
- **Major uncertainty:** The socio-historical setting is Botha's reconstruction, not an independently attested episode. He notes competing form and setting proposals; no date or named opponent is asserted here. His BHS verse numbering counts the superscription.
- **New record:** `psalm-62-community-address-and-wealth-warning`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers trace the transition from personal prayer to communal ethical instruction without overclaiming the historical setting.

## Psalm 71 — old age and intergenerational witness

- **Existing CKL material:** 0 eligible passage-specific items in the current backlog; broad Psalter context remains in the book object.
- **Research question:** How does the plea concerning old age connect with the speaker's intention to proclaim God's acts to the next generation?
- **Original-context evidence:** The petitioner recalls divine instruction from youth, asks not to be forsaken in old age, and links that request to continued proclamation and praise.
- **Verified source and locator:** Kathrin Liess, “Perspectives on Ageing and Old Age in the Old Testament,” in Christian Alexander Neumann, ed., *Old Age before Modernity: Case Studies and Methodological Perspectives, 500 BC–1700 AD* (Heidelberg University Publishing, 2023), 165–183, especially 181–182. Full open-access chapter consulted at [Heidelberg University Publishing](https://doi.org/10.17885/heiup.1086.c14939).
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 71:17–21 joins the speaker's long experience of divine teaching with a request to live on so God's power can be proclaimed to coming generations.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Liess reads old age as a setting for continuing praise and transmission of remembered divine action, not as withdrawal from communal life.
- **Major uncertainty:** This is a literary and theological interpretation of the petitioner, not biographical evidence about a historical speaker or proof of a uniform Israelite social role for older people.
- **New record:** `psalm-71-old-age-and-generational-witness`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how an individual plea can frame intergenerational teaching and praise as part of the poem's purpose.
