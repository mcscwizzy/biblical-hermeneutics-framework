Each packet below answers a bounded question; a common source identity is reused only when it is the same work.

## Psalm 55 — betrayal and city violence

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record. The Psalter’s general source inventory is retained.
- **Question:** Does the poem identify the historical betrayer or event, and how does its urban imagery relate to the personal lament?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 55:9–11 depicts violence and strife in the city; vv. 12–14 identify the betrayer as a companion and familiar friend.
- **Verified source:** Nancy L. deClaissé-Walford, “Human on human violence in the Psalter,” *Review & Expositor* 120, no. 3 (issue August 2023; first online March 5, 2024), 213–220, Psalm 55 discussion, especially 213–215. Official open-access article: <https://doi.org/10.1177/00346373241229788>. Full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and limits:** DeClaissé-Walford says the specific occasion is virtually impossible to recover. She surveys exilic and David/Ahithophel proposals and presents Bail’s feminist hearing as a possible interpretive voice, not as an identified historical speaker.
- **Record:** `psalm-55-betrayal-and-historical-setting-limits`.
- **Reader relevance:** Readers can attend to private betrayal and public disorder together without converting the lament into a report about a named event.

## Psalm 60 — communal defeat and later placement

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record. Reuses the already-present bibliography ID `verde-psalms-trauma-2022`; no duplicate source entry was created.
- **Question:** Does the Davidic heading establish the poem’s original campaign or date?
- **Textual observation:** The poem voices communal defeat and a plea for rescue; its heading associates it with David and Edom, while the body does not name a battle or date.
- **Verified source:** Danilo Verde, “From Healing to Wounding: The Psalms of Communal Lament and the Shaping of Yehud’s Cultural Trauma,” *Open Theology* 8, no. 1 (2022), 345–361, §1, 347–349, and §2, especially 350–353. DOI/full text: <https://doi.org/10.1515/opth-2022-0208>. Full text inspected.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Verde considers Psalm 60’s historical date disputed, reads its Edom language and Book II location as supporting post-exilic use, and treats the superscription as a theological recontextualization. This does not date the poem’s first composition or identify its first military event.
- **Record:** `psalm-60-communal-defeat-and-book-context`.
- **Reader relevance:** Keeps superscription, poem, and later Psalter placement in their separate chronological roles.

## Psalm 66 — collective and individual praise

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** Why does the poem move from a communal “we” to an individual “I”?
- **Textual observation:** Plural speech and communal memory characterize vv. 1–12; singular vows, offerings, and testimony begin at v. 13.
- **Verified source:** Lodewyk Sutton, “Practising piety in a (post-) pandemic time: A spatial reading of piety in Psalm 66 from the perspectives of memory and bodily imagery,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 77, no. 4 (2021), article a6883, section “Understanding piety in Psalm 66.” DOI/full text: <https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v77i4.6883>. Full open-access article inspected.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Sutton reads vv. 1b–12 as collective praise and vv. 13–20 as individual praise, with Exodus memory and cultic imagery connecting the voices. His post-exilic setting concerns final Psalter shaping, not the poem’s original date; his pandemic application is modern reception and is not included as ancient context.
- **Record:** `psalm-66-collective-and-individual-praise`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the change of speaker visible and helps explain how public memory and personal testimony work together.

## Psalm 75 — proposed Hebrew wordplay (limited access)

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** Is there a Hebrew wordplay linking the poem’s language of “mountains” and divine raising?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 75:5–8 contrasts arrogant power with God’s judgment, bringing one low and raising another.
- **Verified source:** Joseph E. Jensen, “Psalm 75: Its Poetic Context and Structure,” *The Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 63, no. 3 (2001), 416–429. Bibliographic record and abstract: <https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/gcd:6295312>; JSTOR issue record confirms title and pages: <https://www.jstor.org/stable/i40150112>.
- **Access limitation:** Only the abstract-level description and journal records were accessible; the full article was not inspected. The item therefore records Jensen’s proposal as provisional and does not assert its philological details as consensus.
- **Record:** `psalm-75-proposed-wordplay-in-judgment-poem` (low confidence).
- **Reader relevance:** Offers a specific lexical question to check against the Hebrew while making the access limitation explicit.

## Psalm 80 — vine imagery and an ambiguous representative

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** Does the “man at your right hand” / “son of man” refer to Israel as a whole or to a royal leader?
- **Textual observation:** A communal restoration refrain frames the damaged-vine image and the appeal for a strengthened man/son figure.
- **Verified source:** Andrew Streett, *The Vine and the Son of Man: Eschatological Interpretation of Psalm 80 in Early Judaism* (Fortress Press, 2014), chapter 1, “Psalm 80 in Its Historical Context,” 15–48, especially publisher sample pages 16–17. Publisher sample: <https://ms.augsburgfortress.org/downloads/9781451472066Chapt1sample.pdf>; publication record: <https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt22nm6dd>.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Streett discusses both people-as-vine and leader/king readings and considers the king a likely referent. The ambiguity is retained. Later Jewish, Christian, or messianic reception does not establish the original referent or date.
- **Record:** `psalm-80-vine-lament-and-son-ambiguity`.
- **Reader relevance:** Guides readers through the poem’s movement from remembered planting to devastation and petition without collapsing original context into later reception.

## Psalm 81 — proposed hinge lines

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** Do Psalm 81:6c and 11c form independent poetic lines or continue the preceding cola?
- **Textual observation:** The Masoretic grouping can be read as tricola; Weber separates the final clauses of vv. 6 and 11 as short, semantically open lines.
- **Verified source:** Beat Weber, “Monocola, Ambiguity and Propositional Density: Poetic Art, Technique and Rhetoric in Psalm 81,” *Journal of Northwest Semitic Languages* 50, nos. 1–2 (2024), 177–187, especially 180–181. University of Pretoria full text: <https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstreams/248a188b-b452-41d1-8d4f-203fd41d5675/download>. Full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Weber argues the two lines act as hinges between larger parts, while acknowledging debate about isolated cola and Masoretic colometry. This is a literary reading, not a manuscript variant; no festival or composition date is inferred.
- **Record:** `psalm-81-hinge-lines-and-colometry-proposal`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the transitions between praise, divine speech, and admonition easier to follow while exposing the lineation question.

## Psalm 97 — kingship within Psalter Book IV

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** What changes when Psalm 97 is read in its current position among Psalms 93–100?
- **Textual observation:** “YHWH reigns” opens the poem; earth, heaven, Zion, and the righteous respond, and other gods appear in the poem’s imagery.
- **Verified source:** Mark W. Hamilton, “Narrating YHWH’s Kingship in Psalms,” *Kairos: Evangelical Journal of Theology* 17, no. 2 (2023), 135–147, especially the discussion of Book IV and the imagined world, 139–144. Open article: <https://doi.org/10.32862/k.17.2.2>.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Hamilton reads the present sequence as a liturgical narrative of divine kingship but distinguishes current arrangement from the origins and dates of individual psalms. This is not proof of a single enthronement festival.
- **Record:** `psalm-97-kingship-and-book-four-narrative`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects the poem’s cosmic and communal audience with its current literary sequence, without turning imagery into a physical cosmology claim.

## Psalm 100 — worship and the kingship sequence

- **Existing CKL:** no passage-eligible child record.
- **Question:** How does the concluding summons to all the earth function after the kingship and warfare language of Psalms 93–99?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 100 calls all the earth to shout, serve, enter the gates with thanksgiving, and praise YHWH’s enduring goodness and faithfulness.
- **Verified source:** D. G. Firth, “Transformation of war language in the worship of all the earth in Psalm 100,” *Acta Theologica*, supplement 32 (2021), 380–391. DOI and open full text: <https://doi.org/10.18820/23099089/actat.sup32.21>.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Firth traces links with Joshua and the surrounding psalms and proposes that conquest language is transformed into joyful worship. This is a literary interpretation, not a claim that Psalm 100 records a specific procession, date, or historical ceremony.
- **Record:** `psalm-100-worship-and-the-psalter-sequence`.
- **Reader relevance:** Lets readers compare the universal summons and sanctuary language with the current collection’s kingship theme.
