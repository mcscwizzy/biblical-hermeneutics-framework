## Psalm 12 — deceptive speech, the poor, and YHWH's word

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific claim or evidence item at baseline; no exact Psalm 12 anchor found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How does the poem relate abusive speech to the plight of the poor, and what does its contrast with YHWH's words contribute?
- **Verified source:** Phil J. Botha, “Pride and the Suffering of the Poor in the Persian Period: Psalm 12 in its Post-Exilic Context,” *Old Testament Essays* 25/1 (2012), 40–56; full SciELO PDF inspected, especially pp. 46–48 and 52–54. [Article PDF](https://scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v25n1/03.pdf).
- **Textual observation:** The speaker contrasts false, boastful human speech with YHWH's tested words and the promise to rise because of the poor and needy's groaning (BHF Psalm 12:2–7).
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha reads Psalm 12 within a wisdom-shaped literary response linking Psalms 9–14 with Proverbs 30:1–14. He interprets YHWH's reliable words against exploitative human speech and proposes a Persian-period horizon for the final form.
- **Major uncertainty:** Botha acknowledges that a specific date cannot be assigned. The proposed literary relationship, direction of influence, and socioeconomic setting remain reconstructions, not independently established history. His verse numbering counts the superscription as v. 1; BHF uses body-verse numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-12-speech-poor-and-wisdom-polarity`; source `botha-psalm12-poor-and-speech-2012`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how the prayer ties the words people use to social vulnerability, then contrasts those words with the promise the poem calls reliable.

## Psalm 28 — a second mood shift at the ending

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific claim or evidence item at baseline; no exact Psalm 28 anchor found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** Does the psalm's movement into praise resolve the opening lament, or does the closing verse renew petition?
- **Verified source:** Armand Barus, “Universal justice: Poetic-affective criticism of Psalm 28,” *Verbum et Ecclesia* 44/1 (2023), a2809; full open-access article inspected, especially “Structure of composition” and “Changes in textual mood.” [DOI and article](https://doi.org/10.4102/ve.v44i1.2809).
- **Textual observation:** The poem moves from a plea, to praise/confidence, and then closes with requests that YHWH save, bless, shepherd, and carry the people (BHF Psalm 28:1–9).
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Barus reads vv. 1–5 as lament, vv. 6–8 as praise, and v. 9 as a renewed prayer, yielding a second mood shift. He notes that other interpreters classify the final verse as praise or prayer.
- **Major uncertainty:** Classification of v. 9 and the poem's social setting remain debated. No single temple rite or historical occasion is established by this reading.
- **New record:** `psalm-28-lament-praise-and-final-petition`; source `barus-psalm28-mood-shift-2023`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers notice that communal confidence at the center does not remove the final request for help.

## Psalm 35 — battlefield and courtroom imagery

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific claim or evidence item at baseline; no exact Psalm 35 anchor found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** How do military conflict and legal accusation work together across the poem?
- **Verified source:** Daniel Simango, “A Contextual and Canonical Reading of Psalm 35,” *Old Testament Essays* 35/3 (2022), 433–452; full open-access article and PDF inspected, particularly §C on structure and §D.1–2 on genre and life-setting. [DOI/full text](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2022/v35n3a5).
- **Textual observation:** The petitioner asks YHWH to contend as warrior and judge; pursuit and defense language is followed by violent witnesses and false testimony, then renewed calls for vindication and public praise.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Simango treats recurring vows of praise and thanks as stanza markers and reads the opening as foregrounding battle, the middle as a lawsuit, and the ending as combining both. He also reports an alternative “royal prayer for international crisis” classification focused on military language.
- **Major uncertainty:** Genre and original life-setting remain unsettled. The superscription and proposed Davidic correspondences do not establish authorship or a specific event. Simango counts the superscription as v. 1; BHF uses body-verse numbering.
- **New record:** `psalm-35-battlefield-and-courtroom-movement`; source `simango-psalm35-structure-and-genre-2022`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the poem's demands for rescue and vindication legible as intertwined military and legal metaphors rather than competing explanations.

## Psalm 38 — body imagery and a plea for wholeness

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific claim or evidence item at baseline; no exact Psalm 38 anchor found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** What does the poem's distributed body language contribute beyond a list of symptoms?
- **Verified source:** Dirk J. Human, “An injured and sick body – Perspectives on the theology of Psalm 38,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 78/1 (2022), a7783; full open-access article inspected, especially “Psalm 38 – A prayer for wholeness” and “Embodiment of an injured life.” [DOI/full text](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v78i1.7783).
- **Textual observation:** The speaker names flesh, bones, wounds, bent posture, heart, eyes, mouth, and feet; the opening plea is matched by a renewed plea for help at the close (BHF Psalm 38:1–22).
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Human reads the varied body-part references as a portrayal of the sufferer's injured body and the closing plea for YHWH's presence as a prayer for wholeness and restored relations.
- **Major uncertainty:** Human notes that poetic body images may be literal, figurative, or symbolic. The poem does not identify a specific disease or medical cause. His article counts the superscription as v. 1, so its body references vv. 2–23 correspond to BHF vv. 1–22.
- **New record:** `psalm-38-embodied-distress-and-prayer-for-wholeness`; source `human-psalm38-body-imagery-2022`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects bodily language with the petition's relational and communal dimensions without turning a poem into a clinical record.

## Psalm 45 — sound and oral performance

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific claim or evidence item at baseline; no exact Psalm 45 anchor found in the Psalms object.
- **Research question:** What can the opening's crafted-speech image and Hebrew sound patterns contribute to translation and reading aloud?
- **Verified source:** Ernst R. Wendland, “‘My tongue is the stylus of a skilled scribe’ (Ps 45:2c): If so in the Scriptures, then why not also in translation?,” *Verbum et Ecclesia* 34/1 (2013), article 777, 8 pages; full open-access PDF inspected, especially pp. 1–3 and 5–6. [DOI/PDF](https://doi.org/10.4102/ve.v34i1.777).
- **Textual observation:** The poet announces a good composition for the king and likens his tongue to a skilled scribe's stylus or pen, making crafted speech the opening image (BHF Psalm 45:1–2).
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Wendland highlights parallel lineation, rhythmic balance, tricola, and recurring alliteration as contributors to rhetorical force and oral articulation. His Chewa examples focus on translation practice.
- **Major uncertainty:** The analysis does not establish one required rendering for all languages. Wendland's proposal that the psalm was publicly declaimed in a royal context is not independent evidence of a particular performance event. His Psalm 45:2 notation counts the superscription; the opening colon maps to BHF body v. 1.
- **New record:** `psalm-45-sound-and-performance-of-royal-poem`; source `wendland-psalm45-sound-and-rhetoric-2013`.
- **Reader relevance:** Encourages readers to hear a royal poem's rhetorical and acoustic artistry alongside its meaning.
