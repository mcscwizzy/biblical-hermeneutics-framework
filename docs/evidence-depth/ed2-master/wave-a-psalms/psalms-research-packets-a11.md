# Wave A research packets — A11

A11 addresses the eight remaining Psalm chapters with no eligible passage-specific CKL material after A10. Eight narrowly framed records were added. Each remains draft/in-review and requires human scholarly review.

## Psalm 58 — the opening word and ancient-version readings

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Why do translations and ancient versions differ over the first Hebrew word of the poem?
- **Verified source and locator:** Christopher T. Begg, “Ps 58,2a: A Forschungsbericht and a Proposal,” *Ephemerides Theologicae Lovanienses* 64, no. 4 (1988), 397–404; publisher abstract and bibliographic record, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2143/ETL.64.4.556410). The full article was inaccessible.
- **Textual observation:** The abstract describes a long-running dispute about the opening word and distinguishes the Masoretic vocalization from readings reflected in ancient versions. Begg’s source notation counts the superscription; source Psalm 58:2a maps to BHF Psalm 58:1a.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** The record reports only the abstract’s survey of alternatives. It does not claim to have read or evaluated Begg’s proposal or the underlying witnesses.
- **Major uncertainty:** Which reading best explains the Hebrew and versional evidence remains unresolved here. Low confidence is used; no original reading is selected.
- **New record:** `psalm-58-opening-word-and-versional-readings`.
- **Reader relevance:** Explains why the opening line is translated differently and separates a versional reading from an established Hebrew text.

## Psalm 76 — Zion hymn and an unspecified battle

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10. Earlier research had deferred an addition because the located sources did not support a direct passage-specific claim.
- **Research question:** Does Psalm 76 identify one historical battle?
- **Verified source and locator:** Stephen John Smith, *The Conflict Between Faith and Experience and the Editing of Psalms 73–83* (PhD dissertation, Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, 2019), ch. 5, “Psalms 74–76,” pp. 92–107, especially “Psalm 76,” pp. 97–98. [Repository dissertation](https://repository.sbts.edu/bitstream/handle/10392/5814/Smith_sbts_0207D_10519.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 76 names Judah, Israel, Salem, and Zion and uses victory language, but names no opponent, ruler, or dated campaign. Smith’s verse numbering counts the superscription: his Psalm 76:2–13 corresponds to BHF Psalm 76:1–12.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Smith treats the poem as a generalized divine-warrior/Zion hymn and reads it within Psalms 74–76. The possible collection-level exilic crisis is an editorial-historical reconstruction, not a date or battle established by Psalm 76 itself.
- **Major uncertainty:** Genre and editorial setting are interpretive proposals; the historical referent of the battle imagery is not specified.
- **New record:** `psalm-76-zion-hymn-and-unspecified-battle`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers resist turning victory imagery or a superscription into an unsupported campaign identification.

## Psalm 138 — the Davidic heading in Book V

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** How may Psalm 138’s Davidic heading shape its reading in the final Psalter without proving historical authorship?
- **Verified source and locator:** Carine Botha, “The role of David in the composition and redactional grouping of the final davidic Psalter,” *Verbum et Ecclesia* 42, no. 1 (2021), article a2291, sections “David in Book V of the Psalter” and “David in the final davidic Psalter.” [Full open-access article](https://verbumetecclesia.org.za/index.php/ve/rt/printerFriendly/2291/4964) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 138 bears a Davidic heading and opens with a first-person vow of praise. Botha treats Psalms 138–145 as a final Davidic grouping and explicitly cautions that such headings do not prove Davidic authorship.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha reads the superscriptions as part of the Psalter’s editorial framing and portrays David as a dependent priest-king. This is a final-form proposal, not a recovered author biography.
- **Major uncertainty:** The headings’ editorial significance and the psalm’s compositional history are debated; no date or historical authorship is claimed.
- **New record:** `psalm-138-praise-in-final-davidic-psalter`.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a way to notice the heading’s role in Book V while keeping canonical presentation separate from origin.

## Psalm 140 — Selah and poetic segmentation

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Do the three Selah placements help mark the poem’s sections and petition movement?
- **Verified source and locator:** Ashley Elizabeth Lyon, *An Analysis of Selah in Antiquity* (PhD dissertation, University of Birmingham, 2018), “Psalm 140,” printed pp. 145–146. [Full dissertation](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/8625/1/Lyon18PhD.pdf) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Lyon notes Selah at source Psalm 140:4, 6, and 9 and analyzes a five-part structure. Her numbering counts the superscription, mapping the Selah locations to BHF Psalm 140:3, 5, and 8.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Lyon treats the placements as section markers and reads the petitions as moving toward increased assurance. She also discusses alternatives, including possible displacement of a marker.
- **Major uncertainty:** The structural divisions are proposals; the word Selah’s performance or musical meaning is not established by these placements.
- **New record:** `psalm-140-selah-and-petition-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how a repeated textual marker can shape the poem’s movement without overstating what is known about ancient performance.

## Psalm 141 — suffixes and the rendering of ʿalilot

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Who is referred to by the plural suffixes, and how should ʿalilot be rendered?
- **Verified source and locator:** Th. Booij, “Psalm 141: A Prayer for Discipline and Protection,” *Biblica* 86, no. 1 (2005), 97–106; publisher abstract and bibliographic record. [Peeters publication record](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php/secure/poj/secure/counter/RSS/secure/COUNTER/RSS/secure/POJ/content.php?id=3191700&journal_code=BIB&url=article) inspected; the full article was inaccessible.
- **Textual observation:** The abstract assigns the plural suffixes in its verses 4–6 to the “workers of mischief,” proposes “fabrications” for ʿalilot, and reads ʿod as “in the end.” Its numbering counts the superscription, so the cited unit maps to BHF Psalm 141:3–5.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Booij argues that these readings connect the petitioner’s request for discipline with the wrongdoers’ intrigues. His wider national-distress setting is not adopted from the abstract alone.
- **Major uncertainty:** The full grammatical argument and alternatives could not be evaluated. The item is low-confidence and reports only the accessible abstract.
- **New record:** `psalm-141-suffixes-and-fabrications`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the effect of pronoun reference and lexical choice visible while preserving the proposal’s limits.

## Psalm 142 — the cave heading and historical limits

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Does the cave superscription identify one recoverable episode in David’s life?
- **Verified source and locator:** Jerome L. Skinner, *The Historical Superscriptions of Davidic Psalms: An Exegetical, Intertextual, and Methodological Analysis* (PhD dissertation, Andrews University, 2016), “Psalm 142: Companion to Psalm 57,” pp. 198–200. [Full dissertation](https://andrews.elsevierpure.com/ws/portalfiles/portal/39958235/The%20Historical%20Superscriptions%20of%20Davidic%20Psalms_%20An%20Exegetical.pdf) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The heading associates David with a cave but names no cave or episode; the prayer itself does not identify a pursuer or event.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Skinner proposes reading Psalm 142 as a thematic companion to Psalm 57 and treats the cave framing as capable of evoking more than one episode, rather than fixing one historical referent.
- **Major uncertainty:** The superscription’s historicity, literary purpose, and relation to a particular episode remain uncertain.
- **New record:** `psalm-142-cave-superscription-and-historical-limits`.
- **Reader relevance:** Distinguishes a heading’s literary frame from proof of one narrative event.

## Psalm 143 — competing readings of its later use in Galatians

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Does the wording of Psalm 143:2 provide a premise for Paul’s argument in Galatians 3:10?
- **Verified sources and locators:** Peter C. Moore, “Psalm 143.2 and the Argument of Galatians 3.10,” *New Testament Studies* 68, no. 4 (2022), 461–473, publisher abstract only, [Cambridge record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0028688522000091); Michael Bachmann, “Gal 2.16c und die Logik von 3.10(–12),” *New Testament Studies* 70 (2024), 125–130, especially pp. 125–127, [full open-access article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DEBEB4A7DE71AC9502830B13316A2BC3/S0028688523000231a.pdf/gal_216c_und_die_logik_von_31012.pdf).
- **Textual observation:** The Greek wording of Psalm 143:2 is taken up in Galatians 2:16c. Moore argues it also underlies the logic of 3:10; Bachmann accepts the wording’s use in 2:16c but rejects Moore’s proposed premise in 3:10 and analyzes 3:10–12 through two syllogisms.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** The record is later reception history and preserves a named scholarly disagreement. Moore’s position is reported only as stated in the abstract; Bachmann’s German-language full text was inspected.
- **Major uncertainty:** The debate concerns the logical role of the psalm wording in Galatians 3:10, not whether Paul uses the wording in Galatians 2:16c. No NE-9C/NE-10 relationship was added or changed; `related_objects` and `related_evidence` remain empty.
- **New record:** `psalm-143-in-galatians-argument-debate`.
- **Reader relevance:** Demonstrates how reception can be studied critically without turning an interpretive proposal into a reviewed cross-passage bridge.

## Psalm 144 — royal victory as dependent on YHWH’s rescue

- **Existing CKL material:** No eligible passage-specific record after A10.
- **Research question:** Does the royal speaker present victory as autonomous royal power or as dependent on divine rescue?
- **Verified source and locator:** Erich Zenger, “The Composition and Theology of the Fifth Book of Psalms, Psalms 107–145,” *Journal for the Study of the Old Testament* 23, no. 80 (1998), 77–102, footnote 50 and accompanying discussion of Psalm 144. [Publisher-hosted article](https://doi.org/10.1177/030908929802308005) inspected; full-text access is restricted, but the relevant footnote is visible.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 144 combines royal battle language, a statement about human frailty, a new song, and a request for deliverance. Zenger treats it as the final royal psalm before Psalm 145.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Zenger argues that Psalm 144 rereads Psalm 18 so that royal victory becomes supplication: the king wins only because YHWH rescues him. This is his literary-theological proposal, not a historical campaign report.
- **Major uncertainty:** The redactional relationships are proposals; the poem does not date or identify a particular battle.
- **New record:** `psalm-144-reworked-royal-victory-song`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers see the poem’s royal imagery alongside its emphasis on human limitation and dependence.
