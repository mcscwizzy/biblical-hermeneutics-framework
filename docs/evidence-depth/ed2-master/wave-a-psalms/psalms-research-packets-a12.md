# Wave A research packets — A12

A12 revisited eight contextual-only Psalm chapters. The ED-1A census found no passage-anchored claims, typed evidence items, or passage-specific interpretive notes in these chapters. The records below add a specific research question to each; they remain draft/in-review and require human scholarly review.

## Psalm 4 — discourse shifts and stanza divisions

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; zero passage-anchored claims and typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** Do shifts in address help explain the poem’s movement from petition to confidence?
- **Source and locator:** Eliathan Carvalho Leite, “Psalm 4: Religiosity and Bible Hebrew Poetry,” *GIS - Gesture, Image and Sound - Anthropology Journal* 7, no. 1 (2022), article e184847, English abstract. [DOI and abstract](https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.2525-3123.gis.2022.184847) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 4 shifts among address to YHWH, speech about YHWH, and address to human hearers.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Leite’s abstract proposes stanza divisions based on inflected-person shifts and describes a dialogue among three cosmic figures. The abstract does not identify those figures.
- **Uncertainty and access limit:** The article PDF could not be retrieved through the available reader, and the Portuguese body was not independently reviewed. The item reports only the abstract’s structural proposal at low confidence.
- **Record:** `psalm-4-dialogue-and-stanza-delimitation`.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a concrete discourse question to test against the poem without inventing the identities of its figures.

## Psalm 16 — Torah-wisdom and contentment

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** Does the language of YHWH as portion, counsel, and path function as a prayer of distress or as wisdom teaching about contentment?
- **Source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “True Happiness in the Presence of YHWH: The Literary and Theological Context for Understanding Psalm 16,” *Old Testament Essays* 29, no. 1 (2016), 61–84, especially pp. 65–66 and 78–79. [Full open-access article](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v29n1/04.pdf) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The speaker calls YHWH his portion, describes counsel during the night, and concludes with the path of life and pleasures in YHWH’s presence.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha argues that the poem works as Torah-wisdom instruction and belongs with Psalms 1, 19, and 23 in the Psalms 15–24 cluster. He contrasts this with Hossfeld and Zenger’s supplication reading.
- **Uncertainty:** Genre and collection history are disputed. Botha’s proposed chronology is not used as an established date.
- **Record:** `psalm-16-wisdom-contentment-and-psalter-context`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the psalm’s language of inheritance and divine guidance legible as a possible account of the good life.

## Psalm 18 — a difference from 2 Samuel 22

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** What does the Psalm’s opening line contribute when compared with its song parallel in 2 Samuel 22?
- **Source and locator:** Kwang-Hyung Lee, “The Translation of ‘I Love You, Lord’ of Psalm 18:2 and Its Theological Meaning: Focusing on Its Comparison with 2 Samuel 22,” *Korean Journal of Old Testament Studies* 28, no. 3 (2022), 217–245, English abstract and final English summary, pp. 217, 243–245. [Publisher record and PDF](https://doi.org/10.24333/jkots.2022.28.3.217) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Lee reports that the Psalm’s opening line, using the unusual qal form of *rhm*, has no counterpart in 2 Samuel 22.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Lee argues that the Psalmic version clarifies and modernizes parts of the Samuel song and interprets *rhm* alongside Aramaic forms and Psalm 116.
- **Uncertainty and access limit:** The full article is principally in Korean; only its English abstract and summary were used to state the proposal. Lee numbers the superscription as verse 1, so source Psalm 18:2 maps to BHF Psalm 18:1. Editorial direction is not independently established.
- **Record:** `psalm-18-distinctive-opening-and-samuel-parallel`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows that the parallel poems differ at the point of the Psalm’s opening address and that the difference has lexical and interpretive consequences.

## Psalm 23 — hospitality and “return” to YHWH’s house

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** Does the host scene affect whether the final verb means “return” to the house of YHWH or “dwell” there?
- **Source and locator:** Andrew E. Arterbury and William H. Bellinger, “‘Returning’ to the Hospitality of the Lord: A Reconsideration of Psalm 23,5–6,” *Biblica* 86, no. 3 (2005), 387–395, publisher abstract. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.2143/BIB.86.3.3191719) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Verses 5–6 move from a prepared table, anointed head, and cup to the speaker’s future in YHWH’s house.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** The authors’ abstract argues that ancient hospitality best illuminates the host image and supports translating the final verb as “I shall return.”
- **Uncertainty and access limit:** Full text was restricted. The record reports this abstract-level translation proposal, not a consensus or settled lexical result.
- **Record:** `psalm-23-host-hospitality-and-return-reading`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects the meal imagery to a real translation choice while keeping “return” clearly attributed.

## Psalm 27 — the verb בקר in the temple request

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** Does בקר in Psalm 27:4 describe inspecting an offering or reflecting in YHWH’s presence?
- **Source and locator:** Philippus J. Botha, “Psalm 27:4 – To reflect in his temple: Communion with YHWH as the culmination of the journey of life,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 77, no. 1 (2021), article a6623, especially the discussion of verse 4, pp. 1–8. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v77i1.6623) inspected; the publisher’s erratum was also checked for the issue number.
- **Textual observation:** Verse 4 pairs gazing on YHWH’s kindness with בקר in the temple phrase; the poem later repeats seeking YHWH’s face and seeing divine goodness.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha argues for reflection/communion in YHWH’s presence rather than priestly inspection of a sacrifice, using the verse’s parallelism and Psalms 16, 23, and 25–34.
- **Uncertainty:** The lexical reading remains an interpretation that challenges an earlier cultic proposal. Botha argues that a particular cultic rite need not be the controlling setting.
- **Record:** `psalm-27-bqr-temple-reflection-reading`.
- **Reader relevance:** Puts a difficult Hebrew verb and competing ritual/literary readings in view.

## Psalm 29 — evaluating Ugaritic comparisons

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** What comparative evidence should readers examine before claiming that Psalm 29 directly borrows from a Canaanite hymn?
- **Source and locator:** Joanna Töyräänvuori, “Psalm 29 and Methodological Triangulation: What Can Ugaritic Parallels and Iconographic Motifs Add to the Interpretation of a Psalm?,” *Biblische Notizen*, Neue Folge 186 (2020), 51–74, publisher abstract and issue contents. [Publisher page](https://www.herder.de/bn-nf/hefte/archiv/2020/186-2020/psalm-29-and-methodological-triangulation-what-can-ugaritic-parallels-and-iconographic-motifs-add-to-the-interpretation-of-a-psalm/) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 29 uses storm and thunder imagery and names Lebanon and Sirion, features often compared with Ugaritic storm-god material.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Töyräänvuori proposes comparing the Psalm with both the Baal Cycle (KTU 1.4 VII 25–52) and Baal’s enthronement hymn (KTU 1.101), alongside Syrian glyptic depictions.
- **Uncertainty and access limit:** The publisher abstract verifies the proposed comparison method; the full article and its conclusions were not accessible. No direct dependence or origin as a Baal hymn is asserted.
- **Record:** `psalm-29-ugaritic-comparison-method`.
- **Reader relevance:** Replaces a one-parallel shortcut with a traceable set of texts and images that would need comparative evaluation.

## Psalm 31 — the speaker’s times and spirit in YHWH’s hand

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** How do the two “hand” sayings connect trust in YHWH to the psalm’s threats and its place among Psalms 25–34?
- **Source and locator:** Phil J. Botha, “‘Into Your Hand, I Commit My Spirit …’ (Ps 31:6): The Purpose of Psalm 31 as a Part of the Cluster of Psalms 25–34,” *Journal of Northwest Semitic Languages* 49, no. 1 (2023), 1–33, text table pp. 4–5 and §4.5, pp. 24–25. [Full article PDF](https://repository.up.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/1e87ce99-a96a-4970-bc74-1c85c25e9d48/content) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** The speaker commits the spirit and times to YHWH’s hand, while the poem also speaks of an enemy’s hand and hidden nets.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Botha reads the prayer as testimony of rescue that exhorts a community to trust YHWH. He proposes that the surrounding cluster’s conflict is mainly ethical and social in post-exilic Judah rather than a series of historical military episodes.
- **Uncertainty:** Botha’s post-exilic group setting is an editorial-historical reconstruction. The source counts the heading as verse 1; Psalm 31:6 and 31:16 map to BHF Psalm 31:5 and 15.
- **Record:** `psalm-31-hand-imagery-and-cluster-exhortation`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers track the repeated hand metaphor and distinguish the poem’s words from the scholar’s reconstructed community setting.

## Psalm 32 — confession and instruction as a single poem

- **Existing CKL material:** Contextual-only coverage; no passage-anchored claims or typed evidence items in the A11 census.
- **Research question:** Do the poem’s repeated words bind confession and wisdom instruction into one composition, or is the wisdom framing a later layer?
- **Source and locator:** J. Henk Potgieter, “The structure and homogeneity of Psalm 32,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 70, no. 1 (2014), article a2725, Table 1 and sections on stanza structure and textual strategy, pp. 1–6. [Full open-access article](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v70i1.2725) inspected.
- **Textual observation:** Verses 1–5 repeat words for sin and forgiveness; verses 6–11 repeat terms for protection, joy, and the righteous, with direct instruction at verse 8.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** Potgieter argues that two parallel stanzas (1–5 and 6–11) form one wisdom teaching poem about confession and accepting YHWH’s guidance.
- **Uncertainty:** The article acknowledges form-critical proposals that posit a thanksgiving core with later wisdom additions. Potgieter’s unity and genre argument is attributed rather than presented as consensus.
- **Record:** `psalm-32-two-stanza-wisdom-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Gives readers a way to test whether repetition links the personal confession to the communal instruction that follows.
