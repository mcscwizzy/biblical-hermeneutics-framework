# Wave A — Psalms research packets, batch A3

Baseline: Psalms 7, 11, 14, 17, 21, 26, 33, 43, 49, and 83 had no eligible passage-specific CKL item before this batch. The Psalter-wide source and context material remains available; these packets answer narrower reader questions and identify the limits of each source.

## Psalm 7 — does the superscription identify a recoverable event?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** Do the Cush superscription and threat language identify one episode in David’s life?
- **Textual observation:** The Hebrew superscription calls the poem a *shiggaion* and associates it with David and “the words of Cush, a Benjaminite.” The speaker describes pursuers but names no episode in Samuel.
- **Verified sources:** Yitzhak Berger, “The David–Benjaminite Conflict and the Intertextual Field of Psalm 7,” *Journal for the Study of the Old Testament* 38, no. 3 (2014), 279–296. [SAGE bibliographic record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0309089214527206); abstract only, full text inaccessible. Mary J. Obiorah, “Reality of insecurity in Psalm 7 and Christian mission in Nigeria,” *Verbum et Ecclesia* 42, no. 1 (2021), article a2141, “Understanding Psalm 7,” pp. 1–2 of 8. [DOI/full text](https://doi.org/10.4102/ve.v42i1.2141); full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Berger’s abstract relates the poem to David’s restraint toward Saul and other Benjaminites. Obiorah surveys ancient-version readings such as “Cushite” and a proposed connection with the messenger in 2 Samuel 18, while noting the mismatch between the poem’s threat language and a dirge. These are competing reconstructions, not independently verified biographies; Berger’s argument is available here only through the abstract.
- **Record:** `psalm-7-cush-superscription-and-setting-limits`.
- **Reader relevance:** Separates the superscription’s explicit wording from proposals that identify Cush or the prayer with a named narrative episode.

## Psalm 11 — how does the poem answer pressure to flee?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** How do temple and throne imagery shape the speaker’s response to danger?
- **Textual observation:** The opening voice urges flight; the speaker confesses refuge in YHWH. The poem places YHWH in the holy temple and on a heavenly throne, and closes with the upright seeing YHWH’s face.
- **Verified source:** Gert T. M. Prinsloo, “Suffering as Separation: Towards a Spatial Reading of Psalm 11,” *Old Testament Essays* 28, no. 3 (2015), 777–806, especially 782–784. [DOI/full open article](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2015/v28n3a13); full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Prinsloo reads the poem’s movement between danger and refuge as a spatial argument: YHWH’s temple and heavenly throne form the center of presence and judgment. This is a literary reading of the poem’s rhetoric, not evidence for a recoverable worship rite.
- **Record:** `psalm-11-refuge-and-spatial-argument`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows how the imagery answers the flight proposal without claiming that the psalm documents a specific ritual setting.

## Psalm 14 — what can the parallel with Psalm 53 establish?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained. No relationship edge was authored.
- **Research question:** Does textual comparison establish the direction of literary reuse between Psalms 14 and 53?
- **Textual observation:** The poems share most of their sequence and wording but differ at several descriptions of the wicked, terror, and their fate. Their divine-name forms also reflect the Psalter’s Elohistic collection.
- **Verified source:** Phil J. Botha, “Psalm 53 in canonical perspective,” *Old Testament Essays* 26, no. 3 (2013), 583–606, especially 584–585 and the comparative discussion. [Full open article](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/ote/v26n3/04.pdf); full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Botha argues that Psalm 53 adapts Psalm 14 for its placement among Psalms 52–55 and a wisdom-oriented reading of David’s story. He acknowledges and rejects the reverse-direction proposal. Close literary reuse is observable; the poems alone do not establish who composed them, when, or which came first.
- **Record:** `psalm-14-psalm53-revision-debate`.
- **Reader relevance:** Makes the textual differences visible while keeping the proposed direction of reuse attributed and contestable.

## Psalm 17 — which readings at verses 11 and 14 are secure?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** Do recent proposals at two difficult verses represent attested readings or editorial judgments?
- **Textual observation:** The study treats vv. 11 and 14 as textual or translational difficulties. Its English abstract reports a conjecture at v. 11 and a preference for the Masoretic *qere* at v. 14.
- **Verified source:** Stanisław Bazyliński, “Annotazioni sul testo del Salmo 17 [Notes on the Text of Psalm 17],” *The Biblical Annals* 11, no. 3 (2021), 387–410; English abstract at 387. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.31743/biban.12435); the abstract and metadata were inspected, but the full Italian article was not reviewed.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** The record attributes both decisions to the author and does not claim either is the earliest or original wording. The accessible abstract does not show the full comparison of Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions.
- **Record:** `psalm-17-textual-proposals-verses-11-and-14`.
- **Reader relevance:** Distinguishes a conjectural emendation from an attested Masoretic reading tradition and makes the access limitation explicit.

## Psalm 21 — does “request” echo diplomatic language?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** Does the unusual word for the king’s request show that the psalm borrowed the language of royal correspondence?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 21:2 says that the king’s request was not withheld. Sheveka’s abstract identifies the Hebrew noun as a hapax and compares the phrase with Amarna Letter EA 9.
- **Verified source:** Avi Sheveka, “A Trace of the Tradition of Diplomatic Correspondence in Royal Psalms,” *Journal of Semitic Studies* 50, no. 2 (2005), 297–320. [Oxford Academic record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/jss/fgi039); abstract only, full text inaccessible.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Sheveka proposes an echo of diplomatic “brotherhood” language and a court-scribal adaptation. Because only the abstract was accessible, the underlying letter analysis was not checked here; direct borrowing, a royal-chancery origin, and the psalm’s date remain unestablished.
- **Record:** `psalm-21-request-language-diplomatic-proposal`.
- **Reader relevance:** Highlights a lexical feature and a possible audience association without turning an abstract-level proposal into a historical fact.

## Psalm 26 — is the temple prayer shaped by wisdom language?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** How do “walking in integrity” and temple imagery work together in the poem?
- **Textual observation:** The speaker opens and closes with a claim to walk in integrity, asks YHWH to test heart and kidneys, distinguishes from violent company, and declares love for YHWH’s house and altar. The poem is not an alphabetic acrostic.
- **Verified source:** Annette Potgieter, “Walking wisely: Sapiential influence in Psalm 26,” *HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies* 69, no. 1 (2013), article 1378, especially the discussion of concentric structure and walking the way of YHWH. [DOI/full open article](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v69i1.1378); full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Potgieter argues that repeated walking language and the poem’s structure display sapiential influence in a poem often classed as cultic. Her proposed Persian-period composition date is not adopted; neither the wording nor the article establishes a precise performance setting.
- **Record:** `psalm-26-walking-integrity-and-wisdom-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Connects ethical conduct and temple imagery while separating literary interpretation from a proposed date or ceremony.

## Psalm 33 — what do the “sea” and “deep” signify?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** Do the paired waters evoke creation alone, or a wider remembered story of divine control?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 33:6–9 pairs YHWH’s word and breath in creation with the sea gathered like a heap and the deep placed in storehouses; the surrounding lines say that YHWH spoke and it came to be.
- **Verified source:** Johan Stoltz, *Psalm 33: A Literary, Contextual and Theological Analysis* (MTh dissertation, University of Pretoria, 2018), ch. 3, 67–68, discussion of v. 7. [Full dissertation](https://repository.up.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/fa1dad6a-7bea-43b3-8d47-68712e9d6fae/content); full thesis inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Stoltz treats “sea” and “deep” as a merism for all waters under YHWH’s control and proposes creation, Exodus, and Canaan-entry resonances; he notes Job 38 as an alternative comparison. These are literary proposals in a master’s dissertation, not evidence of direct borrowing from Ugaritic poetry or of a composition date.
- **Record:** `psalm-33-sea-and-deep-imagery`.
- **Reader relevance:** Helps readers compare the image’s creation language with Israel’s remembered deliverance traditions while keeping the proposed resonances qualified.

## Psalm 43 — do the shared refrain and manuscript report prove original unity?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained. No relationship edge was authored.
- **Research question:** What supports reading Psalms 42–43 as one poem, and what remains uncertain?
- **Textual observation:** Psalm 43 has no separate superscription and repeats the refrain about the downcast soul and hope in God found twice at the end of Psalm 42. Its five verses move from petition toward praise and hope.
- **Verified source:** Daniel Simango, “Psalms 42–43 and 137: A Timeless Balm for Modern Trauma,” *Old Testament Essays* 38, no. 3 (2025), 6–7, note 18 and discussion of the structure. [DOI/full open article](https://doi.org/10.17159/2312-3621/2025/v38n3a3); full article inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Simango reads the two psalms as a three-stanza individual lament divided by the refrain. His report that some ancient copies do not separate them cites Gerald H. Wilson and does not identify witnesses; this item relies on the observable heading and refrain, not independent manuscript verification. Original compositional unity remains unsettled.
- **Record:** `psalm-43-refrain-and-stanza-structure`.
- **Reader relevance:** Shows the literary basis for a unified reading without presenting a secondhand manuscript report as verified textual evidence.

## Psalm 49 — how does the poem organize its warning about wealth and death?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** What does a proposed chiastic structure place at the center of this wisdom poem?
- **Textual observation:** The poem addresses all peoples, announces a wisdom saying, warns against trust in wealth, and contrasts human mortality with God’s power to redeem from Sheol.
- **Verified source:** J. David Pleins, “Death and Endurance: Reassessing the Literary Structure and Theology of Psalm 49,” *Journal for the Study of the Old Testament* 21, no. 69 (1996), 19–27. [SAGE bibliographic record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/030908929602106902); abstract only, full article inaccessible.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** The abstract argues that a chiastic reading focuses the poem’s wisdom on wealth, death, and endurance. The item does not reconstruct a detailed strophic map or infer a settled Israelite doctrine of the afterlife from language whose interpretation is disputed.
- **Record:** `psalm-49-wisdom-poem-and-chiastic-focus`.
- **Reader relevance:** Provides a bounded structural proposal while making clear what could not be verified without the full article.

## Psalm 83 — does the nation list identify one historical coalition?

- **Existing CKL:** No eligible passage-specific item; broad Psalter context retained.
- **Research question:** Does the list of ten peoples, including Assyria, fit a single named battle or period?
- **Textual observation:** The poem names neighboring peoples as a coalition against Israel and includes Assyria; it names no date, ruler, battle, or treaty.
- **Verified source:** David C. Mitchell, *The Message of the Psalter: An Eschatological Programme in the Book of Psalms* (PhD thesis, University of Edinburgh, 1995), ch. III, 73–74. [Full dissertation](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/8890bdea-708a-4451-87f3-ce3863bab929/content); full thesis inspected.
- **Interpretation and uncertainty:** Mitchell argues that the list, particularly Assyria’s inclusion, is difficult to reduce to one local alliance from Jehoshaphat’s time and reads it archetypically and eschatologically. His proposed historical horizon is not a settled composition date, and the poem does not identify a single coalition or timetable.
- **Record:** `psalm-83-coalition-imagery-and-setting-limits`.
- **Reader relevance:** Prevents the familiar named-battle proposal from being mistaken for something the poem independently dates.
