## Research and curation

The wave mixed city, household, and artifact context with broad concept and word-study links. Passage-specific classifications were selected only where a passage appeared in both endpoint objects’ authored Scripture anchors. The 36 curated identities are recorded with exact CKL endpoints, relationship metadata, direction, explanation, supporting passages, and resolvable CKL sources in the wave-6 topic files.

Passage-level textual links include Galatia in Acts 18:23; Macedonia in 1 Thessalonians 1:7–8 and Philippians 4:15–18; Passover in Exodus 12:1–14; the birth narrative in Luke 2:1–20; Adam as a `τύπος` in Romans 5:12–19; the Abba/adoption pairing in Romans 8:14–17; the Psalm 2 term *māšîaḥ*; the Greek *Christos* in Luke 24:25–27; and the holiness vocabulary in Leviticus 19 and 1 Peter 1. The two Abba/adoption and two Lachish edges preserve their separate authored directions.

Historical and archaeological links are deliberately narrower than the CKL labels. Isaiah 36 contextualizes the Lachish reliefs without making them an illustration or independent proof of Isaiah. The gate/relief edges do not identify the excavated gate as the exact gate pictured. The Siloam inscription to Hezekiah-tunnel context edge was held during acceptance. Its attached CKL citations did not include usable epigraphic or archaeological support for the claim that the inscription records the tunnel cutting. 2 Kings 20:20 names a waterworks but does not name the inscription or settle the tunnel’s date; the archaeological claim is not counted as a supported curation.

The first-century stepped street provides topographical context for Luke’s Jerusalem approach, not evidence that Jesus used this route. The IAA report describes a street running from the Siloam Pool to the Temple Mount and assigns its initial construction to the first century CE. [Israel Antiquities Authority, “Jerusalem, City of David, the Stepped Street”](https://hadashot.iaa.org.il/report_detail_eng.aspx?id=26274&mag_id=135).

For Isaiah 53, the servant-to-Messiah, servant-to-sacrifice, and honor/shame-to-cross links are marked as interpretive associations. Their explanations preserve the disputed identity and original setting of the servant, distinguish later Christian reception from the poem’s literary setting, and avoid treating “honor and shame” as a universal social formula. [Moyaert, “Who Is the Suffering Servant?”](https://academic.oup.com/fordham-scholarship-online/book/20670/chapter-abstract/179996941), [Lund University record for *The Suffering Servant: Isaiah 53 in Jewish and Christian Sources*](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/the-suffering-servant-isaiah-53-in-jewish-and-christian-sources/).

The Corinthian legal-context pair is contextualized through 1 Corinthians 6:1–8. The curation records the debate over formal Roman litigation and private arbitration without choosing between them. Roi Ziv’s 2024 article argues for private arbitration and discusses papyrological parallels; his proposal is presented as an alternative, not consensus. [Ziv, “First Corinthians 6.1–6: Roman Court or Private Arbitration?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/first-corinthians-616-roman-court-or-private-arbitration/EFB64225E87B9FE612B3679D5BE73CAA).

Patronage is connected to the Pauline collection in 2 Corinthians 8 only as a comparative framework. The curation retains Paul’s language of voluntary giving, partnership, recipient dignity, and equality, and does not identify the collection as a patron–client transaction. [Barclay, *Paul and the Gift*](https://academic.oup.com/yale-scholarship-online/book/13071/chapter-abstract/166294170), [Downs, *The Offering of the Gentiles*](https://academic.oup.com/book/4078/chapter-abstract/145774319).
