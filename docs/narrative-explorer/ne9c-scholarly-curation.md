Unmatched, out-of-scope, and unresolved relationships stay `uncategorized`.

### Research notes by cluster

**Creation, Eden, new creation, resurrection.** Genesis 1-3 is a literary sequence of cosmic ordering, human vocation, garden life, and expulsion. Hebrew *bārāʾ* (“create”) marks divine creative action in Genesis 1, while *ʿāśâ* (“make/do”) and *yāṣar* (“form”) appear in related creation language; lexical overlap alone does not prove a later text's dependence. Revelation 21 uses Greek *kainos* (“new”) and *poiō* (“make”) and reuses prophetic restoration imagery as well as creation language. In 1 Cor 15:20, *aparchē* (“firstfruits”) directly describes the risen Christ in relation to those who belong to him; Romans 8 supplies a clearer creation-liberation frame. The `interpretive_association` label on resurrection therefore marks a canonical synthesis, not an explicit phrase in 1 Corinthians.

The CKL Genesis object includes primary Genesis/Psalm/John/Colossians/Revelation sources and Nahum Sarna's *The JPS Torah Commentary: Genesis*. Mark B. Stephens' chapter on creation and new creation in Revelation provides a focused account of Revelation's creation motif ([Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34244/chapter-abstract/290347848)). The Yale Open Courses lecture introduces ancient Near Eastern creation material as context ([Genesis 1-4 lecture](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-3)). Comparative creation traditions are contextual evidence; shared motifs by themselves do not prove direct borrowing.

**Divine council and heavenly assembly.** Psalm 82:1 opens with *ʾĕlōhîm* standing in the *ʿădat-ʾēl* (“assembly of El/God”) and includes *ʾĕlōhîm* and “sons of the Most High” language. Deut 32:8 has a material textual variant: the Masoretic Text reads “sons of Israel,” while 4QDeut-j supports “sons of God” and the Old Greek has “angels of God.” The variant matters for textual history; it does not by itself settle every question about Israelite divine plurality. Job 1-2 stages the “sons of God” (*benê hāʾĕlōhîm*) in a heavenly court; the figure called *haśśāṭān* functions as an accuser/adversary in that scene. Ugaritic *pḫr ʾilm* (“assembly of gods”) is a comparative phrase from a different corpus and historical setting.

CKL primary/source records are `ancient-divine-assembly-imagery` / `psalm-82-hebrew-text`, `deuteronomy-32-text`, `job-1-2-text`, and the two linked evidence items. Its bibliography includes Mark S. Smith's *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle* (1994; [Brill](https://brill.com/display/title/2665)) and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001; [Oxford University Press](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-origins-of-biblical-monotheism-9780195167689)), and Theodore J. Lewis' *The Origin and Character of God* (2020; [Oxford University Press](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-origin-and-character-of-god-9780190072539)). These are influential comparative works, not unanimity. Eugene Ulrich's CKL record for 4QDeut-j keeps the manuscript evidence distinct. Recent work also argues for a particular reading of Psalm 82's figures; Shane M. Thompson's 2024 article is one proposal, not a consensus ([JBL article DOI](https://doi.org/10.15699/jbl.1432.2024.4)).

**Tabernacle, temple, sacred space, presence.** Hebrew *miškān* (“dwelling”), *miqdāš* (“sanctuary”), and *ʾōhel môʿēd* (“tent of meeting”) describe related but not interchangeable aspects of the wilderness sanctuary. Exodus 25:8 gives the dwelling purpose; Exodus 40:34-38 narrates cloud and glory. First Kings 8:4 explicitly names the tent of meeting in the temple dedication narrative; 8:10-13 describes the cloud filling the building. John 1:14 uses *eskēnōsen* (“dwelt/pitched a tent”) for the Word among people, a later Christian reception of dwelling imagery. Revelation 21:22 uses *naos* (“temple”) and says no temple is seen because God and the Lamb are the city's temple. The New Jerusalem image has been read as an all-encompassing sanctuary and in other ways. Eyal Regev surveys the alternative-temple imagery in Revelation ([chapter](https://academic.oup.com/yale-scholarship-online/book/31345/chapter-abstract/289537201)); the [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology chapter](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheTempleintheChristianBible) discusses the new city and competing temple readings. CKL source records include Carol Meyers, *Exodus* (Cambridge, 2005; [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/exodus/0BBD532445DFE6B3D2BA9CB53E3E6906/listing)) and Michael B. Hundley, “Sacred Space and Common Space” (Oxford, 2020; [Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34254/chapter-abstract/290406086)).

**Covenants, exile, and restoration.** Hebrew *berît* is translated “covenant,” but covenant texts do not all use one form or carry one legal/historical model. Genesis 12, 15, and 17 have distinct promise and covenant scenes. Exodus 19 and 24 frame Sinai covenant; 2 Samuel 7 gives a dynastic promise to David, which later texts explicitly connect to covenant (for example Ps 89 and 2 Sam 23:5). Jeremiah 31:31 names a *berît ḥădāšâ* (“new covenant”); Hebrews 8:8-12 quotes the passage using Greek *diathēkē*. Ezekiel 36:24-28 describes gathering, cleansing, a new heart, and spirit but does not use Jeremiah's “new covenant” formula in those verses. The graph therefore classifies Hebrews' quotation as later reception and Ezekiel's link as thematic. Continuity/discontinuity arguments in Hebrews are debated; Susan Haber describes both elements and a critical perspective on Judaism in her study ([JSNT article](https://doi.org/10.1177/0142064X05057915)). The registry does not endorse a supersessionist conclusion.

**Exodus, exile, restoration, and new Exodus.** Isaiah 40-55 uses wilderness-road, return, and consolation imagery; interpreters disagree whether “new Exodus” names a deliberate literary pattern, a metaphorical return, or an overly broad modern category. The CKL's `new-exodus-theme → exile-theme` edge is only `related-concept` with no note, so it remains uncategorized. This batch does not turn shared restoration language into a claim of dependence. Rikki Watts explicitly frames the interpretive issue in “Consolation or Confrontation?” ([Tyndale Bulletin](https://www.tyndalebulletin.org/article/30506-consolation-or-confrontation-isaiah-40-55-and-the-delay-of-the-new-exodus)); Daniel Lynwood Smith cautions that “new Exodus” has been used broadly in New Testament scholarship ([JSNT article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1476993x14549915)). A later batch should inspect the exact authored note, anchors, and scope before reconsidering that edge.

**Kingship, Messiah, and kingdom.** Hebrew *māšîaḥ* (“anointed”) and Greek *christos* are titles whose referents depend on passage and period; *malkût* and *basileia* name rule/kingdom, not one fixed political arrangement. Psalm 2 has a royal setting and sonship language; Acts 2:22-36 receives Psalms 16 and 110 in a Christian resurrection/exaltation argument. Daniel 7's Aramaic *bar ʾĕnāš* (“one like a human being”) has collective and individual interpretations, with debate about angelic/divine and messianic readings. Daniel Boyarin surveys collective and divine readings rather than reducing the issue to one answer ([Harvard Theological Review article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0017816012000478)). Susan Gillingham studies Psalm 2's Jewish and Christian reception in *A Journey of Two Psalms* ([Oxford University Press](https://academic.oup.com/book/9436)). These sources support preserving royal setting and later reception as distinct layers.

## Held for later research

- `ancient-divine-assembly-imagery → deuteronomy` is a broad book-level `textual-variant-context` edge. It is not classified: Deut 32:8's witnesses differ, the passage is in a poetic song, and a book-wide edge would erase passage and textual scope. Keep the MT, Qumran, and Greek witnesses distinct.
- `new-exodus-theme → exile-theme` has the generic authored label `related-concept` and no note. Shared restoration vocabulary is insufficient to decide the intended relationship.
- Generic `related`, `related-concept`, book-level relations, and shared vocabulary remain uncategorized unless a separately reviewed exact edge receives direct support and passage scope.
- The referents in Psalm 82, the composition/date/history of Psalm 82, and the extent of continuity between Israelite and Ugaritic religious language remain open. Comparison is not dependence.
- The meaning of the New Jerusalem's “no temple” statement, the nature of covenant fulfillment, the immediate setting and later messianic interpretation of royal texts, and the referent of Daniel 7's human-like figure remain debated.

## Implementation and reader behavior

The curated overlay is applied after existing passage projection and reciprocal-edge resolution. It changes only the projected classification and adds a curation payload; it does not change connection IDs or CKL content. The existing NE-9B evidence-target eligibility and navigation path remain in use. Narrative Explorer now shows a reader-facing reason, textual observation, supporting passages and CKL source record IDs, plus an interpretive uncertainty note. Following a connection carries that explanation into the existing evidence trail. Generic and out-of-scope edges keep their prior category.
