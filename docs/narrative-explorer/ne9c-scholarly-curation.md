Curation requires an exact match on relationship kind, source ID, target ID, original CKL label, original note, and weight. Unmatched, out-of-scope, and unresolved relationships stay `uncategorized`.

### Research notes by cluster

**Creation, Eden, new creation, resurrection.** Genesis 1-3 is a literary sequence of cosmic ordering, human vocation, garden life, and expulsion. Hebrew *bārāʾ* (“create”) marks divine creative action in Genesis 1, while *ʿāśâ* (“make/do”) and *yāṣar* (“form”) appear in related creation language; lexical overlap alone does not prove a later text's dependence. Revelation 21 uses Greek *kainos* (“new”) and *poiō* (“make”) and reuses prophetic restoration imagery as well as creation language. In 1 Cor 15:20, *aparchē* (“firstfruits”) directly describes the risen Christ in relation to those who belong to him; Romans 8 supplies a clearer creation-liberation frame. The `interpretive_association` label on resurrection therefore marks a canonical synthesis, not an explicit phrase in 1 Corinthians.

The CKL Genesis object includes primary Genesis/Psalm/John/Colossians/Revelation sources and Nahum Sarna's *The JPS Torah Commentary: Genesis*. Mark B. Stephens' chapter on creation and new creation in Revelation provides a focused account of Revelation's creation motif ([Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34244/chapter-abstract/290347848)). The Yale Open Courses lecture introduces ancient Near Eastern creation material as context ([Genesis 1-4 lecture](https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145/lecture-3)). Comparative creation traditions are contextual evidence; shared motifs by themselves do not prove direct borrowing.

**Divine council and heavenly assembly.** Psalm 82:1 opens with *ʾĕlōhîm* standing in the *ʿădat-ʾēl* (“assembly of El/God”) and includes plural *ʾĕlōhîm* and “sons of the Most High” language. The referents are disputed: proposals include heavenly beings, human rulers or judges, national patrons, and powers rhetorically demoted by the poem. Deut 32:8 in the Song of Moses has distinct witnesses: the Masoretic Text reads *bənê yiśrāʾēl* (“sons of Israel”), 4QDeut-j preserves *bənê ʾĕlōhîm* (“sons of God”), and the Old Greek reads *angelōn theou* (“angels of God”). This is a textual-critical observation; it does not by itself settle the earliest wording or a complete model of Israelite divine plurality. Job 1-2 is prose narrative whose repeated *benê hāʾĕlōhîm* scene frames the test of Job; *haśśāṭān* functions as an accuser/adversary in that scene, not automatically as the later fully developed figure Satan. Job 38:7 repeats “sons of God” in a poetic creation speech alongside morning stars, but shared wording across distinct genres does not establish dependence. Psalm 89:5-7 belongs to a royal-covenant psalm and speaks of the assembly of holy ones and *bənê ʾēlîm* (“sons of God/divine beings”); its praise rhetoric should not be flattened into Psalm 82's judicial scene. Daniel 10 is a Hebrew apocalyptic vision narrated in the third year of Cyrus; *śar* (“prince”) figures associated with Persia and Greece and Michael appear in its conflict imagery. The narrative setting, the Hebrew/Aramaic composition of the book, its Old Greek and Theodotion witnesses, and accounts of its final composition are separate questions. Many critical accounts relate the book's final form to the second-century BCE Antiochene crisis, while traditional accounts accept an earlier setting. Neither position makes Daniel 10 identical to Job's prose court or Psalm 82's poetic judgment. The CKL's Job bibliography includes Carol A. Newsom's *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations* (2009) and C. L. Seow's *Job 1-21*; its assembly records include Mark S. Smith, Theodore J. Lewis, and Eugene Ulrich. Ugaritic *pḫr ʾilm* (“assembly of gods”) is comparative material from a different corpus and historical setting, not Scripture or proof of borrowing.

CKL primary/source records are `ancient-divine-assembly-imagery` / `psalm-82-hebrew-text`, `deuteronomy-32-text`, `job-1-2-text`, and the two linked evidence items. Its bibliography includes Mark S. Smith's *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle* (1994; [Brill](https://brill.com/display/title/2665)) and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001; [Oxford University Press](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-origins-of-biblical-monotheism-9780195167689)), and Theodore J. Lewis' *The Origin and Character of God* (2020; [Oxford University Press](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-origin-and-character-of-god-9780190072539)). These are influential comparative works, not unanimity. Eugene Ulrich's CKL record for 4QDeut-j keeps the manuscript evidence distinct. Recent work also argues for a particular reading of Psalm 82's figures; Shane M. Thompson's 2024 article is one proposal, not a consensus ([JBL article DOI](https://doi.org/10.15699/jbl.1432.2024.4)).

**Tabernacle, temple, sacred space, presence.** Hebrew *miškān* (“dwelling”), *miqdāš* (“sanctuary”), and *ʾōhel môʿēd* (“tent of meeting”) describe related but not interchangeable aspects of the wilderness sanctuary. Exodus 25:8 gives the dwelling purpose; Exodus 40:34-38 narrates cloud and glory. First Kings 8:4 explicitly names the tent of meeting in the temple dedication narrative; 8:10-13 describes the cloud filling the building. John 1:14 uses *eskēnōsen* (“dwelt/pitched a tent”) for the Word among people, a later Christian reception of dwelling imagery. Revelation 21:22 uses *naos* (“temple”) and says no temple is seen because God and the Lamb are the city's temple. The New Jerusalem image has been read as an all-encompassing sanctuary and in other ways. Eyal Regev surveys the alternative-temple imagery in Revelation ([chapter](https://academic.oup.com/yale-scholarship-online/book/31345/chapter-abstract/289537201)); the [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology chapter](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheTempleintheChristianBible) discusses the new city and competing temple readings. CKL source records include Carol Meyers, *Exodus* (Cambridge, 2005; [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/exodus/0BBD532445DFE6B3D2BA9CB53E3E6906/listing)) and Michael B. Hundley, “Sacred Space and Common Space” (Oxford, 2020; [Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34254/chapter-abstract/290406086)).

**Covenants, exile, and restoration.** Hebrew *berît* is translated “covenant,” but covenant texts do not all use one form or carry one legal/historical model. Genesis 12, 15, and 17 have distinct promise and covenant scenes. Exodus 19 and 24 frame Sinai covenant; 2 Samuel 7 gives a dynastic promise to David, which later texts explicitly connect to covenant (for example Ps 89 and 2 Sam 23:5). Jeremiah 31:31 names a *berît ḥădāšâ* (“new covenant”); Hebrews 8:8-12 quotes the passage using Greek *diathēkē*. Ezekiel 36:24-28 describes gathering, cleansing, a new heart, and spirit but does not use Jeremiah's “new covenant” formula in those verses. The graph therefore classifies Hebrews' quotation as later reception and Ezekiel's link as thematic. Continuity/discontinuity arguments in Hebrews are debated; Susan Haber describes both elements and a critical perspective on Judaism in her study ([JSNT article](https://doi.org/10.1177/0142064X05057915)). The registry does not endorse a supersessionist conclusion.

**Exodus, exile, restoration, and new Exodus.** Isaiah 40-55 addresses comfort, return, and restoration through wilderness-road imagery; Isa 43:16-21 recalls YHWH's *derek* (“way”) through the sea and pairs it with a *ḥădāšâ* (“new thing”), wilderness road, and desert water. The exodus imagery is textually visible, while the label “new Exodus” is a scholarly description whose scope is debated. Jeremiah 16:14-15 uses the “days are coming” formula and recalls YHWH bringing Israel up from Egypt before describing future gathering from the north and other lands; it does not quote one identified Exodus verse, and the formula may be conventional. Hosea 2's wilderness renewal and Egypt memory belong to prophetic poetry shaped by the eighth-century northern kingdom's crisis; this is not the later Babylonian deportation. Ezekiel 36-37 speaks from an exilic horizon of regathering, cleansing, new heart (*lēb ḥādāš*) and spirit (*rûaḥ ḥădāšâ*), and national restoration. Ezekiel 37:11 explicitly identifies the dry bones with the whole house of Israel; later resurrection readings are reception, not a replacement for that stated referent. Mark 1:2-3 gives a demonstrable later Greek-scriptural reception of Isaiah 40:3 within a composite opening citation, but no exact authored CKL Mark-to-Isaiah object edge was available for this batch. The new Exodus classification is confined to the Exodus 14 / Isaiah 43 image route. The generic CKL `new-exodus-theme → exile-theme` edge remains uncategorized. Rikki Watts frames the interpretive issue in “Consolation or Confrontation?” ([Tyndale Bulletin](https://www.tyndalebulletin.org/article/30506-consolation-or-confrontation-isaiah-40-55-and-the-delay-of-the-new-exodus)); Daniel Lynwood Smith cautions that “new Exodus” has been used broadly in New Testament scholarship ([JSNT article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1476993x14549915)).

**Kingship, Messiah, and kingdom.** Hebrew *māšîaḥ* (“anointed”) and Greek *christos* are titles whose referents depend on passage and period; *malkût* and *basileia* name rule/kingdom, not one fixed political arrangement. Psalm 2 has a royal setting and sonship language; Acts 2:22-36 receives Psalms 16 and 110 in a Christian resurrection/exaltation argument. Daniel 7's Aramaic *bar ʾĕnāš* (“one like a human being”) has collective and individual interpretations, with debate about angelic/divine and messianic readings. Daniel Boyarin surveys collective and divine readings rather than reducing the issue to one answer ([Harvard Theological Review article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0017816012000478)). Susan Gillingham studies Psalm 2's Jewish and Christian reception in *A Journey of Two Psalms* ([Oxford University Press](https://academic.oup.com/book/9436)). These sources support preserving royal setting and later reception as distinct layers.

## Held for later research

- The review considered 18 additional routes or comparisons: the five curated object edges above and these 13 held candidates.
- `psalms → ancient-divine-assembly-imagery` is a broad book-level edge suppressed by existing projection rules. Psalm 89:5-7 and the target cluster's Psalm 82:1-8 anchors do not provide a common scoped route.
- Evidence `job-sons-of-god-assembly → psalm-82-assembly-language` is held because the two evidence records apply to different passages; the target is not eligible in the same projection.
- Job 38:7's “sons of God” wording and Job 1-2's prose heavenly scene share a phrase across different genres, but there is no authored edge and the wording alone does not demonstrate dependence.
- Psalm 89:5-7 and Psalm 82:1-8 can be compared as distinct assembly language, but the broad Psalms-to-cluster edge is not exposed by passage projection and the poems have different rhetorical settings.
- Deut 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 are a research comparison, not a curated CKL edge. The textual variants in Deut 32 do not settle Psalm 82's referents or establish a common literary model.
- Daniel 10 compared with Deut 32, Psalm 82, Job 1-2, and Psalm 89 remains research-only. Similar heavenly or national language does not create an authored edge, and genre and narrative function differ.
- `jeremiah → restoration-theme`, `hosea → exile-theme`, `isaiah → babylonian-exile`, and `ezekiel → restoration-theme` remain uncurated book-level edges. Their subject matter merits further research, but current projection suppresses top-level book relationships. Hosea's eighth-century northern setting also differs from the later Babylonian deportations.
- `jeremiah → daniel` remains uncurated even though Daniel 9:2 explicitly names Jeremiah. The authored relation is on a book object whose broad relationship list is not projected as a passage edge; no reverse edge or new passage-level relationship was invented.
- `new-exodus-theme → exile-theme` has the generic authored label `related-concept` and no note. Shared restoration vocabulary is insufficient to decide the intended relationship.
- `restoration-theme → exile-theme` likewise has only a generic `related-concept` label and no note. Neither generic link is upgraded by this batch.
- Jeremiah 16:14-15's future-gathering formula and Isa 40-55's wilderness-road imagery were studied, but no literary dependence is claimed solely from shared Exodus motifs.
- Generic `related`, `related-concept`, book-level relationships, and shared vocabulary remain uncategorized unless a separately reviewed exact edge receives direct support and passage scope.
- The referents in Psalm 82, the composition/date/history of Psalm 82, and the extent of continuity between Israelite and Ugaritic religious language remain open. Comparison is not dependence.
- The meaning of the New Jerusalem's “no temple” statement, the nature of covenant fulfillment, the immediate setting and later messianic interpretation of royal texts, and the referent of Daniel 7's human-like figure remain debated.

## Implementation and reader behavior

The curated overlay is applied after existing passage projection and reciprocal-edge resolution. It changes only the projected classification and adds a curation payload; it does not change connection IDs or CKL content. The existing NE-9B evidence-target eligibility and navigation path remain in use. Narrative Explorer shows the reader-facing reason, textual observation, Scripture passages, CKL source records, and interpretive uncertainty. Ancient comparative items such as KTU 1.1-1.2 are labeled as comparative material, not Scripture. Curation confidence, CKL evidence certainty, original CKL temporal layer, and human scholarly approval are separate fields; human approval is explicitly `not_recorded`. Subject detail carries the current passage projection's exact curation annotation into its existing authored-relationship navigation. Generic and out-of-scope edges keep their prior category.
