## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

The chapter traces a gradual return to habitable land. God’s remembering of Noah and the creatures is expressed through action: a wind passes over the earth and the waters subside. Noah then uses the raven and repeated releases of the dove to observe changing conditions outside the ark. After leaving the ark, he builds an altar and offers burnt offerings; the description of a “pleasing” or “soothing” aroma belongs to this account of the sacrifice.

### The Receding Waters

The dates distinguish several stages rather than one instant of recovery: the ark rests, mountain tops become visible, Noah sends birds after forty days, surface water is gone, and only later is the ground dry. The sequence slows the narrative down and shows that resting on the mountains was not yet the same as a dry, inhabitable earth.

*Verses:* Genesis 8:3-14<br>

### Ararat and Leaving the Ark

“The mountains of Ararat” need not identify the modern peak called Mount Ararat. In other biblical usage, Ararat designates the Urartu region or kingdom. The chapter then moves from God’s action toward those preserved in the ark to the command for Noah, his household, and the animals to leave it and return to the land.

*Verses:* Genesis 8:1, Genesis 8:15-19, Genesis 8:4<br>

### Genesis in Its Larger Shape

Genesis 8 belongs to the book’s opening movement, Genesis 1–11, which concerns universal origins. From Genesis 12 onward, the book turns to the ancestral family narratives and eventually ends with Jacob’s household in Egypt. This larger structure helps locate the flood account before the narrative narrows to that family.
