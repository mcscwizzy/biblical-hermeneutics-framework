## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

The chapter moves from entry into the ark to the flood’s full force. Noah’s household enters with animals, including a distinction between clean and unclean animals. The phrase about clean animals is understood either as seven pairs or as seven individual animals, while the earlier instructions had described pairs of each kind. The account then marks its progress carefully: Noah is 600, seven days pass before the waters come, rain lasts forty days and nights, the outbreak is dated to the seventeenth day of the second month, and the waters prevail for 150 days. These details give the narrative a deliberate sequence rather than a single undifferentiated disaster. The opening of the “fountains of the great deep” and the “windows of heaven” presents waters coming from below and above, reversing the boundaries that normally contain them.

### The Flood’s Outcome

The chapter’s climax is not merely the rising of water but the death of humans and land animals outside the ark. Genesis 7:21–23 contrasts this comprehensive loss with Noah and those enclosed with him, making the flood’s grave judgment outcome central to the chapter.

### The Scope of the Flood Language

The description of the waters is intentionally comprehensive: it speaks of the earth or land, the high mountains, and all flesh outside the ark. Readers differ over how this language should be understood. One reason is that the Hebrew word ’erets can mean either “earth” or “land,” depending on context. The chapter’s expansive rhetoric should therefore be taken seriously while the question of scope remains an interpretive one.

### Genesis and Other Flood Traditions

Genesis 7 stands within Genesis 1–11, an ancient Near Eastern literary setting in which creation, flood, genealogy, and city-building traditions were also discussed. A Neo-Assyrian tablet preserves the Standard Babylonian Epic’s account of Uta-napishti surviving a divinely sent flood, offering a concrete comparison for themes developed across Genesis 6–9, including a boat, preservation of life, birds, landing, and sacrifice. Such shared motifs can illuminate cultural questions, but they do not by themselves establish direct borrowing or decide how Genesis should be interpreted.
