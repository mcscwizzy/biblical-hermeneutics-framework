## Candidate Commentary

### Jacob’s fearful return

Jacob’s return is shaped by uncertainty about Esau. When his messengers report that Esau is approaching with four hundred men, Jacob is afraid and divides his people and livestock into two camps so that one might escape if the other is attacked. The account reports Jacob’s fear and precaution but does not state Esau’s purpose. After the night encounter, Jacob crosses on with a limp; the narrative describes the impairment without supplying a medical diagnosis.

*Verses:* Genesis 32:25, Genesis 32:31-32, Genesis 32:4-8<br>

### Prayer and blessing

Jacob’s prayer holds together an immediate danger and the promises he recalls: God commanded him to return, promised him good, and promised offspring too numerous to count. Jacob also calls himself unworthy of the steadfast love he has received and asks for rescue from Esau. Later, he insists on receiving a blessing from the nighttime opponent, receives the name Israel, and yet is not told the opponent’s name. The exchange gives Jacob a blessing without giving him full disclosure or control.

*Verses:* Genesis 32:26-29, Genesis 32:9-12<br>
*Evidence IDs:* g32-blessing-request-and-withheld-name, g32-prayer-unworthiness-command-promise<br>

### Gift, struggle, and Peniel

The livestock sent ahead of Jacob are presented as a minḥah, a substantial gift intended to appease Esau. By separating the droves and having each servant identify Jacob as the sender, Jacob makes the gift arrive in stages. The identity of the nighttime opponent remains interpretively open. The narrative first calls him a man, while Jacob later names the place Peniel because he says he has seen God face to face and survived. Readers may understand the figure in different ways, but the text itself preserves this movement from a humanlike opponent to Jacob’s divine interpretation rather than fully explaining every detail.

*Verses:* Genesis 32:13-21, Genesis 32:24-30, Genesis 32:30-31<br>

### Names, places, and the closing custom

The chapter is marked by place-names and explanations. At Mahanaim, Jacob calls the meeting with God’s messengers “God’s camp”; the possible relation of that name to the later “two camps” is debated. The nighttime crossing of the Jabbok leaves Jacob alone before the struggle. His new name, Israel, is explained through a wordplay about striving with God and with humans, though no single English translation captures every aspect of the name. The final verse explains an Israelite food practice by connecting it to Jacob’s injured hip; it gives the origin-story for the practice, not a full later definition of the exact tissue avoided.

### Genesis in context

Genesis moves from its opening account of universal origins in chapters 1–11 into the ancestral family narratives of chapters 12–50. Genesis 32 therefore belongs within the extended story of Jacob’s household, a story that ultimately concludes with that household in Egypt.
