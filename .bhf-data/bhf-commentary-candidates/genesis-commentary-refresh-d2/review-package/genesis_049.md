## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Jacob gathers all his sons to speak of what lies ahead and then delivers a sequence of short, vivid sayings. The images are not uniform: Issachar is a strong donkey bent to labor, Dan is both a judge and a roadside serpent, Gad is attacked yet counterattacks, and Benjamin is a wolf dividing prey. These compact portraits help explain why the narrator can call them all blessings even though several contain warning, struggle, or loss as well as strength.

### Family, judgment, and blessing

Jacob's words are not merely praise. Reuben is acknowledged as the firstborn and as the beginning of Jacob's strength, yet is told that he will not retain preeminence because of his act against his father. Simeon and Levi are condemned for fierce, cruel anger and are told that they will be divided and scattered. Later, Joseph's blessing uses several titles for God—the Mighty One of Jacob, the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel, the God of Jacob's father, and Shaddai—while describing help and abundant blessing.

*Verses:* Genesis 49:24-25, Genesis 49:3-4, Genesis 49:5-7<br>

Judah is pictured with the strength and settled confidence of a lion, while his brothers are said to praise him and bow before him. Joseph, by contrast, is portrayed as a fruitful branch by a spring: attacked by archers but remaining strong through divine help. The brief cry, “I wait for your salvation, LORD,” interrupts the tribal sayings, and Asher's saying turns instead to rich food and royal delicacies. Together these images give different sons distinct portraits without making their blessings identical.

### A difficult line in Judah's saying

Genesis 49:10 connects Judah with a scepter, a ruler's staff, and the obedience of peoples, but its central Hebrew clause is difficult. Translations and interpretations differ: it may be read as “until Shiloh comes,” “until he comes to Shiloh,” “until tribute comes to him,” or “until that which belongs to him comes.” The uncertainty belongs to this particular clause; the surrounding lines still plainly use imagery of authority, abundant vines and wine, a coastal setting for Zebulun, and Naphtali as a released doe associated with graceful words.

### Genesis in its larger setting

Genesis moves from the universal beginnings of chapters 1–11 to the ancestral family narratives of chapters 12–50, ending with Jacob's household in Egypt. Genesis 49 therefore stands near the close of that family-centered movement, as Jacob addresses the household that the narrative has followed.

Questions about how Genesis came to be written should be kept distinct from what the book itself narrates. Mosaic attribution and compositional models are different proposals, while Genesis does not narrate its own literary formation. Likewise, the absence of a direct archaeological identification cannot by itself prove or disprove every ancestral narrative; archaeological and literary questions are not the same kind of question.
