## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Genesis 18 moves from Abraham’s lavish welcome of unexpected visitors to Sarah’s promised son and then to the question of Sodom’s judgment. Abraham’s hospitality is narrated through concrete acts—water, rest, bread, meat, curds, and milk—rather than presented as a universal rule. Sarah’s laughter acknowledges how improbable the promised birth seems in light of her and Abraham’s age. The later dialogue turns from that promise to whether the righteous may be swept away with the wicked.

### The Appointed Time

The promise is not left indefinite: the visitor names an appointed return and says that Sarah will have a son. This gives the exchange a specific focus. Sarah’s response is not erased; her inward laughter remains part of the scene even as the promise is repeated.

### Questions in the Narrative

The account shifts between “three men,” speech identified with Jehovah, departing visitors, and Abraham remaining before Jehovah. Readers have understood this in different ways: as a divine appearance, as angelic or messenger figures, or as Jehovah accompanied by messengers. The narrative itself preserves that complexity rather than supplying a single explanatory label.

*Verses:* Genesis 18:1-22<br>

When Sarah calls Abraham “my lord” in her private reflection, the phrase belongs to the household rhetoric of this scene and to later scriptural reception of Sarah. It should not be turned into a general claim that women become daughters only by deference to men or that it establishes male superiority.

### Righteousness and Justice

The chapter links Abraham’s calling with teaching his household to do righteousness and justice, while the following conversation asks whether judgment can distinguish the righteous from the wicked. Abraham’s appeal to the Judge of all the earth is therefore not a detached bargaining scene: it develops the concern for justice already named in his household vocation and in the promise concerning the nations.

### Genesis in Context

Genesis 18 belongs to the ancestral family narratives that occupy Genesis 12–50, following the book’s opening movement from universal origins in chapters 1–11. The chapter’s focus on Abraham, Sarah, descendants, household instruction, and the nations belongs within that larger family-centered portion of Genesis.
