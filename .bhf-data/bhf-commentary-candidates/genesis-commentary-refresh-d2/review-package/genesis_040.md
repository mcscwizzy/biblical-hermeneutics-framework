## Candidate Commentary

### Dreams and Joseph’s Interpretation

The two dreams draw on the officials’ own work: the cupbearer handles Pharaoh’s cup and serves him wine, while the baker carries baskets of food. Joseph interprets the three branches and three baskets as three days, but the similar time frame leads to sharply different outcomes—restoration for one official and execution for the other. When the men say they have no interpreter, Joseph explicitly says that interpretations belong to God; this frames his reading of both dreams.

### Royal Offices and Custody

The cupbearer and baker are presented as high-ranking royal servants responsible for drink and food service. Comparable courts had such offices, but these Hebrew titles cannot be matched with certainty to particular Egyptian positions or to identifiable historical individuals. Likewise, the prison is detention under royal authority, not necessarily a modern-style penal institution or an identified archaeological site. Pharaoh’s birthday feast becomes the setting for the reversal: the cupbearer is restored, while the baker is executed; the death term is rendered either “hanged” or “impaled.”

*Verses:* Genesis 40:1-4, Genesis 40:20-22, Genesis 40:3-8<br>

### An Unfinished Deliverance

Joseph asks the restored cupbearer to remember him, but the chapter ends with the official forgetting. That ending postpones Joseph’s release and carries the narrative forward toward Pharaoh’s dreams.

*Verses:* Genesis 40:16-19, Genesis 40:23, Genesis 40:8, Genesis 40:9-15<br>

### Genesis in Its Larger Setting

Genesis moves from the universal beginnings of chapters 1–11 to the ancestral family narratives of chapters 12–50, ending with Jacob’s household in Egypt. Questions about how Genesis took literary form should be kept distinct from the story it tells: Mosaic attribution and compositional models are differing positions, while Genesis itself does not narrate its own literary formation. Similarly, the lack of direct archaeological identification does not by itself prove or disprove every ancestral narrative, since archaeology and literary interpretation address different questions.
