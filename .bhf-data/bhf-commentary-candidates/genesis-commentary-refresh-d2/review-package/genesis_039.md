## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Joseph’s situation reverses sharply: an enslaved foreigner entrusted with an Egyptian official’s household becomes a prisoner, yet the prison keeper also entrusts him with responsibility. The chapter’s “prison” is its setting for confinement and custody; it should not automatically be equated with a modern prison institution. It supplies no trial transcript or full legal procedure, so the account focuses instead on Joseph’s vulnerable status, the accusation’s retelling, and the repeated reversal in his circumstances.

### Power and trust in the household

Potiphar’s wife presses Joseph repeatedly, while Joseph describes her proposal as both a betrayal of his master’s trust and a sin against God. The scene is not presented as a romance: Joseph is an enslaved foreigner facing pressure from a powerful member of the household. His refusal therefore places accountability before God within a markedly unequal relationship.

*Verses:* Genesis 39:2-6, Genesis 39:7-12, Genesis 39:8-12<br>

### The garment and the retelling

The garment functions as the visible object around which the accusation is constructed. After Joseph flees, Potiphar’s wife displays it to the household servants and then recounts the event again to her husband with altered framing. The repeated narration lets the reader notice how her rhetoric shapes the charge.

### Success and reversal

The repeated statement that YHWH is with Joseph frames both his household service and his custody. It helps explain why the chapter can move from entrusted responsibility to accusation and confinement without treating prosperity as a generic promise of material success.

### Within Genesis

Genesis moves from the universal origins account in chapters 1–11 to the ancestral family narratives in chapters 12–50, ending with Jacob’s household in Egypt. Genesis 39 belongs within that larger movement toward Egypt.
