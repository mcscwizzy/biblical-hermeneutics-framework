## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

The chapter moves from the serpent’s challenge to God’s command, through the humans’ eating, to questioning, blame, and distinct pronouncements. The serpent questions the prohibition and denies the announced death; the woman eats and shares the fruit, and the man eats. When questioned, the man points to the woman and the woman to the serpent. The following statements address the serpent, woman, man, and ground separately; the text explicitly calls the serpent and the ground cursed, rather than applying that formula to every statement.

### Shame, Clothing, and Exclusion

The humans’ response after eating sharply contrasts with Genesis 2:25: nakedness that had carried no shame now prompts them to sew fig leaves and hide. God later makes garments for them. The serpent is described here as a crafty creature among the animals God made; Genesis itself does not name it Satan.

*Verses:* Genesis 3:1-5, Genesis 3:14-15, Genesis 3:21, Genesis 3:7-10<br>
*Evidence IDs:* g3-serpent-genesis-characterization, g3-serpent-genesis-characterization:passage-relevance, g3-shame-sewing-and-clothing, g3-shame-sewing-and-clothing:passage-relevance<br>

The chapter links difficult cultivation with mortality: the ground is cursed on the man’s account, labor becomes arduous, and the man will return to the ground from which he was taken. Expulsion from Eden then prevents access to the tree of life; cherubim and a turning flaming sword guard its way. The narrative gives no further description of how that weapon works.

### Questions Raised by the Chapter

The tree is presented as good for food, pleasing to the eyes, and desirable for gaining wisdom. In this account, “knowledge” is therefore more than abstract information: it belongs to the narrated desire, eating, and its consequences. Exactly how its meaning should be defined remains an interpretive question.

*Verses:* Genesis 3:22, Genesis 3:4-7<br>
*Evidence IDs:* g3-tree-knowing-and-desire, g3-tree-knowing-and-desire:passage-relevance<br>
*Synthesis IDs:* syn_interpretive_questions_92b0e3bea720

Genesis 3:15 directly describes conflict between the serpent and the woman, and between their offspring, in terms of striking head and heel. It does not name a particular messiah. Later Jewish and Christian interpretation connected this conflict with adversarial powers, and Revelation later identifies the “ancient serpent” with the devil and Satan; those later readings should not be treated as names Genesis itself supplies.

*Verses:* Genesis 3:1, Genesis 3:15<br>

Cherubim guard the way to the tree of life east of Eden. Later biblical sanctuary texts also associate cherubim with sacred settings, which has led some readers to see Eden as sacred space. That is an interpretation, however; Genesis does not explicitly identify Eden as a temple or describe the cherubim’s appearance.

*Verses:* Genesis 3:15, Genesis 3:24, Genesis 3:8<br>

### Genesis in View

Genesis begins with accounts of universal origins in chapters 1–11, then turns in chapters 12–50 to ancestral family narratives, ending with Jacob’s household in Egypt. Genesis 3 belongs to the opening, universal portion of the book rather than the later family narratives.
