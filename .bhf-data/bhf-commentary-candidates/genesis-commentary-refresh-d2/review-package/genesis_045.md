## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Joseph’s disclosure comes immediately after Judah offers himself in Benjamin’s place. Joseph removes the Egyptian attendants, weeps, and names himself as the brother whom they sold. The brothers’ silence shows the shock of the revelation; the embraces, kisses, conversation, and travel gifts that follow show the movement from the final test toward reconciliation. Joseph does not deny the brothers’ wrongdoing: he recalls it while interpreting his arrival in Egypt as God’s means of preserving life.

### The Famine’s Urgency

Joseph locates this reunion within the famine: two years have already passed, and five more lie ahead without plowing or harvest. This explains the urgency of moving Jacob’s household and sharpens Joseph’s statement that he was sent ahead to keep survivors alive.

*Verses:* Genesis 45:6-7<br>

### Egypt, Goshen, and the Move

The chapter depicts an official welcome: Pharaoh authorizes wagons for the family, promises them Egypt’s good land, and Joseph offers settlement in Goshen near him. Joseph also calls himself “a father to Pharaoh,” alongside his authority over Pharaoh’s household and Egypt. Archaeological research at Tell el-Dabʿa documents Egypt–Levant contact and settlement in particular Middle Kingdom and Middle Bronze Age contexts. That material provides a setting for regional interaction, but it does not identify Jacob’s family, Joseph, or a particular dynasty.

### Genesis in Its Larger Setting

In the larger movement of Genesis, the book shifts from its accounts of universal origins in chapters 1–11 to the ancestral family narratives in chapters 12–50, ending with Jacob’s household in Egypt. Genesis 45 is a decisive step toward that ending because it prepares the family’s move to Egypt.
