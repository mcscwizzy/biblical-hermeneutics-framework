## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Verse 4 uses the toledot formula to mark a transition from the preceding creation account into a scene focused closely on the land, the human, and the garden. Readers differ over exactly how the two units relate, but the shift in focus is clear. Within this garden scene, the human is assigned to work or serve the garden and to keep or guard it; the wording gives a garden vocation without explicitly making the human a priest.

### Humanity, Ground, and Shame

The wording for the human and the ground is closely linked: ha-adam means “the human/man,” and ha-adamah means “the ground/soil.” The human is formed from soil and receives the breath of life. This connection becomes more pointed when Genesis 3 later recalls both difficult cultivation and the human’s return to the ground. The chapter’s final picture of the man and woman naked and unashamed also stands immediately before the later introduction of shame and covering.

### Garden, Rivers, and Partnership

Eden is described as a planted garden with a river that divides into four branches. Although Pishon, Gihon, Tigris, and Euphrates are named, the passage gives no coordinates; Pishon and Gihon remain unidentified. The river names therefore do not provide a recoverable map location for the garden. Likewise, describing Eden as sacred space or as a temple is an interpretation, not an explicit identification made by Genesis 2.

*Verses:* Genesis 2:8-10, Genesis 2:10-14<br>

The partner sought for the human is called ezer kenegdo: a helper corresponding to, or facing, the human. The following language—“bone of my bones” and “flesh of my flesh”—leads into the statement that husband and wife become “one flesh,” joining kinship language to marital union in this narrative.

### Why These Details Matter

The soil-and-breath scene and the closing note of unashamed nakedness establish features that the next chapter will revisit: the ground becomes difficult to cultivate, the human returns to it, and shame and covering enter the story. Reading Genesis 2 with those later echoes in view helps explain why these details are placed so prominently at the beginning and end of the garden narrative.

### Later Reading

A later epistolary argument invokes Adam’s prior formation and Eve’s deception. Interpreters dispute how that use of Genesis supports the command being discussed, including whether its rule is local or universal. That disagreement belongs to the later interpretation; it should not obscure the immediate movement of Genesis 2 itself.

### Genesis in Its Wider Setting

Genesis 1–11 participates in an ancient Near Eastern literary environment where creation, flood, genealogy, and city-building traditions were also discussed. Such comparisons can clarify shared motifs and questions, but they do not by themselves prove direct copying or settle how Genesis should be interpreted. For example, a Babylonian epic tells of divine succession and combat before Marduk orders the cosmos and establishes cultic supremacy; it provides a comparison for Genesis 1’s creation setting without making the accounts equivalent.
