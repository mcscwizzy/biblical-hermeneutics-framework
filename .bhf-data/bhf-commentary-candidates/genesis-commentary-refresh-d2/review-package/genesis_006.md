## Candidate Commentary

### Difficult opening verses

The phrase “sons of God” in verses 1–4 is not explained by the passage itself. It distinguishes these male figures from the daughters of humanity, but their identity and the significance of the marriages remain disputed. Later Second Temple writings, including 1 Enoch and Jubilees, developed an angelic or Watchers interpretation; they show how later readers understood the episode, rather than supplying missing details to Genesis.

Verse 4 does not unambiguously equate the Nephilim with either the children of these unions or the “mighty men” and “men of renown.” The chapter then shifts from this compressed, difficult episode to its stated diagnosis of the world: persistent evil in human intention, corruption, and violence. The account holds inward disposition and public harm together without treating them as the same thing.

### Judgment and preservation

The description of God’s regret and grief uses emotionally vivid human language, framing the judgment as a response to human evil. The ark instructions are correspondingly practical: it is coated with pitch inside and out and includes compartments, an opening, a side door, and three levels. The particular identity of “gopher wood” is uncertain; “cypress” is a proposal, not a settled translation. Before the flood begins, covenant language first appears in Genesis: God promises to establish a covenant with Noah and his household, later specified in Genesis 9 to include Noah’s descendants and living creatures.

*Verses:* Genesis 6:14-16, Genesis 6:18, Genesis 6:6-7<br>

### Later interpretation

A later passage connected with Noah speaks of “spirits in prison,” but its referent and the nature of the proclamation are debated. Interpretations include fallen angels, human beings from Noah’s generation, or other spiritual beings; the proclamation has likewise been understood as victory, judgment, or good news. No single option is textually effortless.

### Genesis and flood traditions

Genesis 1–11 belongs to an ancient Near Eastern literary environment in which creation, flood, genealogy, and city-building traditions were also discussed. This setting can clarify shared motifs and questions, but parallels by themselves do not prove direct copying or settle Genesis’s meaning. Within Genesis as a whole, the opening universal story of origins leads into the ancestral family narratives beginning in chapter 12.

A Neo-Assyrian tablet preserves the Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh account of Uta-napishti surviving a divinely sent flood. It offers a concrete comparison for features developed across Genesis 6–9, including boat construction, preservation of life, birds, landing, and sacrifice after the flood. Shared motifs make comparison worthwhile, but do not establish a single line of direct borrowing.
