## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

Abraham’s presentation of Sarah as his sister is repeated in Gerar, but this episode has its own king, setting, dream warning, and restoration. Abimelech insists that he acted without knowing Sarah was married, and the dream both confirms his claim of integrity and identifies the danger created by the deception.

*Verses:* Genesis 20:1-3, Genesis 20:3-7<br>
*Evidence IDs:* g20-abraham-called-prophet, g20-abraham-explanation-restoration, g20-dream-warning-and-integrity, g20-gerar-wife-sister-repetition, g20-gifts-vindication-and-settlement<br>
*Synthesis IDs:* syn_interpretive_questions_00aa894d87e2

The resolution is public as well as personal: Abimelech returns Sarah, gives Abraham livestock, servants, and silver, and offers him a place to live. These gifts function as a material settlement and public resolution, rather than supplying an exact legal schedule for damages. Abraham is also called a prophet here because he is to pray for Abimelech so that he may live; the title highlights his intercession without requiring later institutional ideas about prophecy.

### Household Healing

The chapter attributes the closing of the women’s wombs in Abimelech’s household to YHWH because of Sarah, and then reports healing after Abraham’s prayer. It gives no medical mechanism; the account presents the household’s condition and restoration as part of the episode’s divine judgment and resolution.

*Verses:* Genesis 20:17-18<br>

### Reading Genesis in Its Larger Setting

Genesis moves from the universal origins narratives of chapters 1–11 into the ancestral family narratives of chapters 12–50, ending with Jacob’s household in Egypt. Genesis 20 belongs within that extended family narrative rather than standing as an isolated account.

*Verses:* chapter/surrounding context<br>
*Evidence IDs:* genesis-literary-movement<br>
*Synthesis IDs:* syn_surrounding_passages_8bf09b72f305

Genesis itself does not narrate how its literary form came to be. Mosaic attribution and compositional models are distinct ways readers have approached that question, while the lack of a direct archaeological identification cannot by itself prove or disprove every ancestral narrative; archaeology and literary interpretation address different questions.
