## Candidate Commentary

### Overview

After the flood, God renews the command to be fruitful and multiply. The passage permits animal food but forbids eating flesh with its blood, and it treats the shedding of human blood as requiring an accounting because humanity is made in God’s image. The opening therefore joins renewed human life in the earth with a strong concern for the value of human life.

### The covenant and the bow

God’s covenant is stated broadly: it includes Noah, his descendants, every living creature, and the earth. Its stated promise is specific—floodwaters will not again destroy all flesh in this way. The bow in the cloud is appointed as the sign of that covenant. The Hebrew word qeshet is the ordinary word for a bow; the narrative’s emphasis is on the bow’s appearance in the cloud as a reminder of the enduring promise.

### Noah’s sons and the post-flood world

The notice naming Shem, Ham, and Japheth does more than identify Noah’s sons. By saying that the people who spread over the earth came from these three, it closes the ark narrative and introduces the family line’s role in the post-flood world.

### The wider flood narrative

Genesis 9 concludes a flood account that can be compared with other ancient Mesopotamian flood traditions. A Neo-Assyrian tablet preserves the Standard Babylonian Epic’s account of Uta-napishti surviving a divinely sent flood. Such comparison highlights shared questions and motifs—including a boat, preservation of life, birds, landing, and post-flood sacrifice—but shared motifs alone do not establish direct borrowing or settle how Genesis should be interpreted.

*Verses:* Genesis 6:1-22, Genesis 7:1-24, Genesis 8:1-22<br>

More broadly, Genesis 1–11 belongs to an ancient Near Eastern literary environment in which creation, flood, genealogy, and city-building traditions were also discussed. That setting can clarify the kinds of questions and shared motifs present in these chapters, while leaving open questions about Genesis’s literary formation and preventing parallels from being treated as proof of direct copying.
