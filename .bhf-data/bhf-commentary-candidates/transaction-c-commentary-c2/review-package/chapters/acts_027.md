The chapter’s account contains detailed knowledge of Mediterranean sailing, from changing winds and soundings to the handling of anchors, cargo, and a storm-driven ship. That detail helps the voyage read as a sustained maritime narrative, though the precise location of the wreck and the extent to which the account depends on eyewitness testimony remain uncertain.

### Acts as a Text

Acts survives in early papyri and major Greek manuscripts, including P45, P53, P74, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and Bezae. These witnesses preserve different portions of the book and sometimes different textual profiles.

The first-person plural language in Acts 27–28 belongs to a pattern also found in Acts 16 and 20–21. What these “we” sections indicate about their source or the book’s authorship remains disputed. Codex Bezae and related witnesses also preserve a notably distinctive form of Acts, so particular readings require case-by-case assessment.

### A Question of Detail and Perspective

Acts 27 contains detailed knowledge of Mediterranean sailing, but two questions remain unsettled: the precise location of the wreck and how directly the first-person account reflects eyewitness dependence. The narrative’s detail should therefore be noticed without claiming more certainty than the evidence permits.

_Passages: Acts 27:1-44_

### Acts as a Preserved and Interpreted Work

Acts survives in early papyri and major Greek manuscripts, including P45, P53, P74, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and Bezae; these preserve different portions and textual profiles. Historical assessment of Acts is best made claim by claim, comparing particular statements with independent evidence rather than assigning the whole work a blanket label of reliable or unreliable.

Acts uses first-person plural narration in several sections, including Acts 27–28, but what this implies about authorship or sources remains disputed. Codex Bezae and related witnesses also preserve a notably distinctive form of Acts, so individual textual readings require case-by-case judgment.
