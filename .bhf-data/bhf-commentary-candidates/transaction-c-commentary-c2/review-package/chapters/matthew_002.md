### Published Commentary

### Reading Matthew’s Scripture Citations

Matthew’s citation of Micah in the Bethlehem scene belongs to a broader New Testament pattern of reusing or echoing Micah in settings involving Bethlehem, shepherding, household conflict, and Davidic expectation. In the flight to Egypt, Matthew’s use of Hosea 11:1 is a figural rereading: it reads Israel’s exodus pattern as meaningful for this later event without denying that Hosea speaks retrospectively of Israel as God’s child. The Jeremiah quotation after the killing at Bethlehem should likewise not be reduced to a simple direct prediction; later uses of Jeremiah include reinterpretation within Scripture, Jewish textual and liturgical reception, New Testament quotation and allusion, christological reading, and later church theology.

_Passages: Matthew 2:5-6, Matthew 2:13-15, Matthew 2:17-18_

Matthew describes eastern magi, a star, gifts, Herod’s alarm, and worship of the child, but does not number the magi or call them kings. The chapter alone narrates Herod’s killing of Bethlehem’s young children. Herodian brutality is independently contextualized more broadly, but this particular event is not independently attested. The opening also places the story under Herodian client rule within a remembered first-century setting shaped by Roman provincial authority, taxation, elite power, and crucifixion.

_Passages: Matthew 2:1, Matthew 2:1-12, Matthew 2:15, Matthew 2:16-18, Matthew 2:17-18_

### Places and movement

Bethlehem is identified as being in Judea; the phrase locates the town rather than suggesting that it belongs to someone. The chapter then traces a purposeful sequence of places: Bethlehem, Egypt, the land of Israel, Galilee, and Nazareth. On the return, Joseph avoids Judea because Archelaus is reigning there in Herod’s place, and the family withdraws to Galilee, where they settle in Nazareth. These linked place names clarify the narrative’s final destination without turning the account into a travelogue.

_Passages: Matthew 2:1, Matthew 2:8-11, Matthew 2:13-15, Matthew 2:19-23, Matthew 2:22, Matthew 2:22-23_

### Reading Matthew’s scriptural quotations

Matthew presents the escape to Egypt as fulfilling “Out of Egypt did I call my son.” One interpretive approach understands this as a figural rereading: Hosea’s words look back on Israel’s exodus, where Israel is called God’s child, while Matthew sees Jesus’ movement from Egypt as echoing that earlier pattern. This reading does not require treating Hosea’s original reference to Israel as erased.

_Passages: Matthew 2:13-15_

The quotation of Jeremiah after the killing at Bethlehem should not automatically be read as a simple one-to-one prediction. Jeremiah has been reread and received in several ways—within biblical interpretation, Jewish textual and liturgical use, New Testament quotation and allusion, and later Christian theology. Keeping these modes distinct helps readers recognize that Matthew’s use can be meaningful without collapsing every later use into direct prediction.

_Passages: Matthew 2:17-18_

The Bethlehem citation describes a coming ruler who will shepherd Israel. Matthew’s use belongs to a broader New Testament pattern of reusing or echoing Micah in settings involving Bethlehem, shepherding, household conflict, and Davidic expectation. That wider pattern is an interpretive observation, not a reason to add details beyond what Matthew itself says here.

_Passages: Matthew 2:5-6_

### Details Matthew does and does not give

Matthew calls the visitors wise-men from the east; it does not number them or call them kings. It narrates their star, gifts, worship, Herod’s alarm, and their return by another route. Matthew alone narrates Herod’s killing of Bethlehem’s young children; although broader Herodian brutality is independently contextualized, this particular event is not independently attested. The chapter also repeatedly frames events with scriptural citations and fulfillment formulas.

_Passages: Matthew 2:1, Matthew 2:1-12, Matthew 2:15, Matthew 2:16-18, Matthew 2:17-18_
