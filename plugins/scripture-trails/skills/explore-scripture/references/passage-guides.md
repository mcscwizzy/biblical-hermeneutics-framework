## 1 Kings (Book Module)

### Purpose

This module helps you approach 1 Kings as the narrative of Solomon, the temple,
and the division of the kingdom — kings measured against covenant fidelity, with
the prophets beginning to dominate. It is a hermeneutic profile, not a commentary:
it tells you what to notice and ask, assuming Core, Genre, and Context are already
loaded. It does not tell you what 1 Kings means.

### When to apply

When interpreting any passage in 1 Kings, on top of **Narrative Genre Module** and the
world of the monarchy in **The Israelite Monarchy**.

### Genre signals

Predominantly **narrative** (**Narrative Genre Module**) organized by **regnal accounts**
with recurring evaluative formulae, interwoven with **temple-building** texts,
court history, and **prophetic** narratives (Elijah). Distinguish the
formulaic regnal frame from the extended story cycles.

### Historical anchors

The **monarchy** (**The Israelite Monarchy**) — Solomon's reign, the split
into northern and southern kingdoms, and their parallel histories — and the
**temple** (**Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**) as the center of worship, judged within the
**covenant** standard that the narrative applies to each king
(**Covenant and Treaty Forms**). The history and chronology are debated; hold them with
appropriate confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for the **regnal evaluation formulae** ("he did what was evil/right in the
eyes of the LORD"), the **synchronized** narration of the two kingdoms, the
**temple-dedication** material, **prophetic narrative cycles**, recurring motifs
(idolatry and the "high places," the word of the LORD fulfilled), and **contrast**
between kings. Do not assume every feature is present in a given passage; look for
them.

### Key interpretive questions

Pose, do not answer:

- How does this passage evaluate the king or kingdom against the covenant standard?
- Is the passage reporting events or commending a response?
- How do the regnal formulae shape the way the narrative judges?
- What role does the temple play in this section's concerns?
- How does the prophetic word function in relation to the kings here?
- What would the ancient audience have assumed about kingship, temple, or "high places" that a modern reader might miss?

### Common misreadings

- Reading the regnal accounts as neutral chronicle rather than as evaluated, selective narrative.
- Confusing the northern and southern kingdoms and their kings.
- Treating narrated royal actions as automatically endorsed or prescribed.
- Settling debated chronology and history by assumption rather than observation.

### Handling uncertainty

The chronology of the kings, the sources behind the regnal accounts, and the
book's composition are debated. Present the range, distinguish observation from
reconstruction, and label confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave
conclusions to the reader.

## Deuteronomy (Book Module)

### Purpose

This module helps you approach Deuteronomy as Moses' covenant address on the edge
of the land — law restated and preached, framed as a binding relationship between
the LORD and Israel. It is a hermeneutic profile, not a commentary: it tells you
what to notice and ask, assuming Core, Genre, and Context are already loaded. It
does not tell you what Deuteronomy means.

### When to apply

When interpreting any passage in Deuteronomy, on top of **Law (Legal and Covenant) Genre Module** — the book
is sustained covenant instruction — and the **narrative** frame
(**Narrative Genre Module**) of Moses' farewell speeches.

### Genre signals

Predominantly **law** (**Law (Legal and Covenant) Genre Module**) in a distinctive **preached, hortatory**
mode — exposition and exhortation, not bare statute — cast as **speeches** within
a thin **narrative** frame (**Narrative Genre Module**). It is widely read against the
form of an ancient **treaty/covenant** document. Embedded **poetry** appears
(e.g., the Song and the blessing of Moses).

### Historical anchors

The **covenant** (**Covenant and Treaty Forms**) is central: many note the book's
resemblance to **ancient Near Eastern treaty** structures
(**The Ancient Near East**) — historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and
curses, witnesses. The composition, date, and setting of Deuteronomy are debated
and tradition-linked; hold them with appropriate confidence
(see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for the **treaty/covenant structure**, **sermonic repetition** and motive
clauses ("so that it may go well with you"), the **Shema** and centralizing
emphases, **blessing and curse** sections, embedded **poetry**, and Deuteronomy's
heavy **reuse of earlier Torah** as well as its dense influence on later Scripture
(see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**). Do not assume every feature governs a given
passage; look for them.

### Key interpretive questions

Pose, do not answer:

- How does this passage function within the covenant address — as motivation, stipulation, blessing/curse, or narrative frame?
- How does it restate, expand, or recast earlier Torah, and why might it do so here?
- What does the treaty/covenant frame contribute to reading this passage?
- What would the original audience, poised to enter the land, have heard that a modern reader might miss?
- How does the preached, hortatory style shape the law's claim on the hearer?
- How is this material taken up later in Scripture?

### Common misreadings

- Reading Deuteronomy as flat legislation while missing its preached, persuasive character.
- Ignoring the treaty/covenant frame that shapes the whole.
- Treating its restatement of earlier law as mere repetition rather than asking why it recasts.
- Settling the contested question of a command's ongoing application by assumption rather than careful, stated reasoning.

### Handling uncertainty

The book's composition and date, its relation to ANE treaties, and how (or
whether) its laws apply to later readers are debated and tradition-linked. Present
the major approaches, separate observation from reconstruction, and label
confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave the conclusion to the reader.

## Job (Book Module)

### Purpose

This module helps you approach Job as a wisdom book that probes the limits of the
usual wisdom answers — innocent suffering, divine justice, and the right to
question God — through an extended poetic debate. It is a hermeneutic profile, not
a commentary: it tells you what to notice and ask, assuming Core, Genre, and
Context are already loaded. It does not tell you what Job means.

### When to apply

When interpreting any passage in Job, on top of **Wisdom Genre Module** (especially its
reflective, probing mode) and **Poetry Genre Module**, within the broader conversation of
the wisdom tradition (**The Wisdom Tradition**).

### Genre signals

A **prose frame** (prologue and epilogue) enclosing a long **poetic debate**
(**Poetry Genre Module**) — cycles of speeches between Job and his friends, the Elihu
speeches, and the divine speeches from the whirlwind. This is reflective or
"protest" **wisdom** (**Wisdom Genre Module**). Whose voice is speaking, and where they
stand in the argument, governs how a line should be weighed.

### Historical anchors

The **wisdom tradition** (**The Wisdom Tradition**) and its debate over whether
the world reliably rewards the righteous, set against the wider **ancient Near
East** (**The Ancient Near East**), which produced comparable reflections on
suffering and justice. Note both likeness and difference without assuming
borrowing. Date, setting, and the book's composition are debated; hold them with
appropriate confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for the **prose–poetry frame**, the **speech cycles** and their structure,
the gap between what the reader knows (the prologue) and what the characters know,
**legal and courtroom** imagery (Job's "case"), dense **nature imagery** in the
divine speeches, **irony**, and the way the **friends voice conventional wisdom**
that the book itself tests. Do not assume every feature is present in a given
passage; look for them.

### Key interpretive questions

Pose, do not answer:

- Who is speaking here, and how does the book itself evaluate their position?
- Does this speech advance, defend, or critique conventional wisdom about suffering?
- What does the reader know (from the prologue) that the characters do not, and how does that shape the passage?
- Is the passage stating the book's own view, or a view it is examining?
- What would the ancient audience have assumed about justice, suffering, and the divine that a modern reader might miss?
- What do the divine speeches actually address, and what do they leave open?

### Common misreadings

- Quoting the friends' speeches as the book's own teaching, missing that the book tests them.
- Forcing a tidy resolution that blunts the book's hard questions.
- Treating the prose frame and the poetic debate by one set of rules.
- Reading isolated lines as freestanding doctrine apart from the speaker and the argument.

### Handling uncertainty

The book's composition (e.g., the Elihu speeches), its date and setting, the
meaning of rare words, and how to read the ending are debated, and the book
deliberately leaves hard questions open. Present the range, distinguish observation
from reconstruction, and label confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave
conclusions to the reader.

A **Gospel** (**Gospel Genre Module**) — theological **narrative** (**Narrative Genre Module**) —
but markedly distinct: organized around selected **signs** and long **discourses**

the Synoptics are debated; hold them with appropriate confidence

Minor Prophets, it is mostly a story about a prophet, told with sustained irony,
that turns on God's mercy toward an enemy city. It is a hermeneutic profile, not a

are already loaded. It does not tell you what Jonah means.

### When to apply

When interpreting any passage in Jonah, on top of **Narrative Genre Module** — the book's
primary mode — rather than reading it as a collection of oracles like the other
Minor Prophets.

### Genre signals

Primarily **narrative** (**Narrative Genre Module**): a tightly plotted story about a
reluctant prophet, carried by **irony** and reversal. It contains a single brief
**oracle** (**Prophecy Genre Module**) and an embedded **psalm** of thanksgiving in poetry
(**Poetry Genre Module**). Read the story's craft closely; the humor and irony are part of
the meaning.

### Historical anchors

The narrative is set in the era of the **Assyrian** empire, with **Nineveh** as the
great enemy city (**The Israelite Monarchy** supplies the monarchic/Assyrian
backdrop). The book's genre and historical character are much debated; hold them
with appropriate confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for pervasive **irony** (the pagan sailors and Ninevites respond better than
the prophet), **hyperbole** and comic exaggeration, **structural symmetry** between
the two halves, the embedded **psalm**, repeated motifs (down/flight, "great"
things, appointed creatures and plant), and the **open-ended question** that closes
the book. Note its reuse of stock prophetic and creedal language
(see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**). Do not assume every feature is present in a given
passage; look for them.

- What major approaches exist to the book's genre and historical character, and what are their trade-offs?
- What would the original audience have assumed about Nineveh and Assyria that a modern reader might miss?

### Common misreadings

- Fixating on the great fish while missing the book's irony and its concern with mercy.
- Treating Jonah as a simple hero to imitate rather than a figure the narrative critiques.
- Settling the debated questions of genre and historicity by assumption rather than observation.

### Handling uncertainty

The book's genre, historical character, date, and purpose are read very

### Genre signals

Prophetic **poetry** (**Poetry Genre Module**, **Prophecy Genre Module**) alternating **judgment**
and **salvation** oracles, with a **covenant lawsuit** (*rîb*) form, **woe**
oracles, and a closing appeal. Identify whether a unit indicts, promises, or pleads.

### Historical anchors

**Judah** (and the threat to both kingdoms) in the eighth century under Assyrian
pressure (**The Israelite Monarchy**), with social injustice and corrupt
leaders judged against the **covenant** standard (**Covenant and Treaty Forms**). Hold the
historical details with appropriate confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for the **alternation of judgment and hope**, the **covenant-lawsuit** scene
("what does the LORD require"), indictment of **leaders, prophets, and the wealthy**,
the **ruler-from-Bethlehem** oracle, and **wordplay** on place-names, with reuse of
shared prophetic traditions (note the overlap with Isaiah's "nations to the
mountain" oracle — see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**). Do not assume every feature is
present in a given passage; look for them.

### Key interpretive questions

Pose, do not answer:

- Is this unit judgment, promise, or covenant lawsuit, and to whom is it addressed?
- What injustice or failure of leadership is being indicted?
- Which images are figurative, and what did they evoke for an eighth-century audience?
- How does the passage relate the present judgment to the promised restoration?
- How does Micah's material overlap with other prophets, and what approaches exist to that relationship?

### Common misreadings

- Reading the famous summary lines (e.g., "do justice, love mercy") apart from the oracle and covenant context that give them force.
- Treating the alternation of judgment and hope as disorder rather than design.
- Flattening the poetry and its wordplay into flat prose.
- Settling debated questions of arrangement and the ruler oracle's reference by assumption rather than observation.

### Handling uncertainty

The arrangement of the oracles, the extent of editing, and the reference of the
ruler oracle are debated and read differently across traditions. Present the major
approaches, distinguish observation from reconstruction, and label confidence
(see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave conclusions to the reader.

## Psalms (Book Module)

### Purpose

This module helps you approach the Psalms as a collection of Hebrew poems used in
worship and prayer. It is a hermeneutic profile, not a commentary: it tells you
what to notice and ask, assuming Core, Genre, and Context are already loaded. It
does not tell you what a psalm means.

### When to apply

When interpreting any psalm or section of the Psalter, on top of
**Poetry Genre Module** and, where relevant, the worship setting in **Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**.

### Genre signals

Hebrew **poetry** throughout (**Poetry Genre Module**), but in several recognizable
**psalm types** — for example lament, praise/hymn, thanksgiving, royal, wisdom,
and trust psalms. The Psalter is also an arranged **collection** (traditionally
five books) with superscriptions; the type and any heading shape how a psalm is
read. Identify the likely type before interpreting.

### Historical anchors

The world of Israelite **worship and the Temple** (**Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**) and the
broader **ancient Near East** (**The Ancient Near East**) for shared poetic
imagery and royal/divine motifs. Superscriptions, authorship, and dating of
individual psalms are debated; treat them carefully
(see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

### Literary features

Look for **parallelism** (the basic engine of Hebrew verse), **imagery** and
metaphor, **structure** (refrains, **acrostics**, **inclusio**), **shifts in
voice and address** (to God, self, congregation, enemies), **emotional movement**
(e.g., from lament to trust), and **quotations or echoes** of other Scripture
(see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**). Do not assume every device is present in a given
psalm; look for them.

### Key interpretive questions

Pose, do not answer:

- What type of psalm is this, and what does that type characteristically do?
- Who is speaking, to whom, and how does the speaker's stance shift?
- Which images are figurative, and what did they evoke in their setting?
- How do parallelism and structure shape the psalm's movement and emphasis?
- What is the psalm's setting or use in worship, so far as it can be discerned?
- Does a superscription or placement in the collection affect the reading?

### Common misreadings

- Treating every poetic image as a literal or technical statement.
- Reading a single line of a parallel pair as an independent proposition.
- Mining individual verses for slogans apart from the psalm's overall movement.
- Assuming a superscription settles authorship, date, or occasion with certainty.
- Forcing a uniform structure onto poems of different types.

### Handling uncertainty

Authorship and dating, the meaning of technical terms in superscriptions, psalm
classification, and original liturgical settings are frequently uncertain.
Present the range, distinguish observation from reconstruction, and label
confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).
