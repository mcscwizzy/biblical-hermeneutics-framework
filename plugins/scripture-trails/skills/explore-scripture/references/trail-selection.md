# Trail Selection: Discover, Evaluate, Rank, Offer

Use this procedure after answering the user's question. Generate candidates
from the passage and its context, then discard weak or distracting ones. The
connection should illuminate the question the user actually asked.

## 1. Find candidates from textual signals

Look for candidates in the passage and the context already needed to answer the
user. Give priority to direct textual signals, while staying open to a relevant
historical, geographical, linguistic, or cultural detail:

1. Direct quotation or unmistakable citation of an earlier biblical passage.
2. An explicit reference to another text, person, event, or scriptural theme.
3. A strong literary allusion supported by multiple signals, such as distinctive
   wording, an unusual image, a recognizable sequence, or a close contextual
   fit.
4. A recurring narrative pattern developed across texts, such as exodus,
   covenant, kingship, exile, temple, creation, or restoration.
5. A canonical development or later biblical interpretation that names or
   demonstrably reuses the earlier passage.
6. A relevant historical or cultural connection: geography, ancient Near
   Eastern context, Second Temple Judaism, or a Greco-Roman setting.
7. A Hebrew or Greek observation that clarifies the text and can be stated
   accurately without relying on a folk etymology.
8. Divine Council imagery only when the passage's language and literary setting
   make it relevant.

Use the [Intertextuality](intertextuality.md) reference for the source method.
Read an earlier passage in its own setting before describing how a later author
uses it. A later interpretation can illuminate that author's reading without
erasing the earlier passage's historical context.

## 2. Decide whether a trail helps

For each candidate, ask internally:

- What wording, structure, image, citation, or contextual evidence supports the
  connection?
- Is another passage directly relevant to the user's question?
- Would following it add a genuinely new insight, rather than repeat the
  answer?
- Does it clarify the passage or the issue the user raised?
- Is the link supported by evidence, rather than a superficial resemblance?

Rank worthwhile candidates by textual support, relevance, novelty, and likely
explanatory value. A candidate does not need to be an intertext: a well-grounded
historical, geographical, language, or cultural observation may clarify the
text. Do not offer a candidate merely because it is available.

## 3. Test each candidate

For each candidate, ask:

- What exact words, images, structure, narrative pattern, or explicit citation
  support the link?
- Does the proposed earlier text fit the wording and context better than a
  generic shared theme would?
- Does the connection clarify the user's passage or question?
- Is the relationship in the text, a historical/cultural parallel, a later
  interpretation, or a more tentative comparison?
- Would the connection still seem persuasive if the shared word or theme were
  removed?

Reject a candidate based only on a common word, broad topic, or resemblance
that cannot be tied to identifiable evidence. Do not treat a speculative type
or a thematic parallel as proof of an author's intention.

## 4. Judge confidence internally

- **Very High:** a direct quotation or unmistakable, explicit citation.
- **High:** a strong allusion supported by multiple converging signals.
- **Moderate:** a plausible textual or thematic relationship with identifiable
  evidence, while other explanations remain possible.
- **Low:** a possible echo or speculative comparison with limited support.

Use these categories to decide how strongly to phrase a trail. Do not show a
score. State the relationship plainly: “quotes,” “explicitly refers to,”
“strongly echoes,” “may recall,” or “shares a theme with.” Qualify uncertainty
where it changes what the user should conclude. A low-confidence possibility
usually does not merit a trail unless the user asks for speculative connections.

Keep explicit quotations distinct from interpretive connections. For example,
the Gospels quote Psalm 22:1 in the crucifixion scene, while Hebrews 2:12 quotes
Psalm 22:22. Hebrews 2:13 then draws on Isaiah 8:17–18. These are related
investigations, but they are not the same quotation or relationship.

Likewise, Genesis 11:4 says the builders want to make a name for themselves,
and Genesis 12:2 records God's promise to make Abram's name great. Invite the
reader to compare the narratives without claiming that Genesis explicitly
explains the Babel builders as resisting a command to spread; that is an
interpretive proposal. Acts 2 does not explicitly cite Genesis 11. A Babel and
Pentecost comparison may be offered as a thematic reading, while noting that
Acts 2 depicts people understanding across languages rather than restoring one
universal language.

## 5. Rank and present

Prefer the few candidates with the clearest evidence and greatest explanatory
value. Simple factual questions may need no trail. When a meaningful connection
exists, normally offer one or two optional directions after the answer. Three
are appropriate when the user asks for alternatives or several relationships
genuinely deserve exploration. During a deep investigation, continue the
requested analysis first; mention a next connection only when it naturally
emerges. Do not force suggestions into every answer.

Each invitation should include:

- A short, interesting description.
- A Scripture reference when there is one.
- Why following it could add a new insight or clarify the present question.
- The relationship type, with a brief uncertainty cue when material.

Use a specific, open question that explains why the connection matters without
prescribing a conclusion. Keep it optional and concise. Avoid generic closings
such as “Would you like to explore more?” If the evidence does not support a
useful trail, end after the answer. If the user chooses one, retain the original
passage and follow that connection in subsequent turns.
