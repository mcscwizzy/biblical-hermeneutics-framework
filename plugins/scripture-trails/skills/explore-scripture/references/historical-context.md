## The Ancient Near East

### Purpose

This module helps you situate Old Testament texts in the world of the ancient
Near East (ANE) — its cosmology, institutions, and cultural assumptions — so you
read the text in its own setting rather than through modern categories. It
supplies background questions to ask; it does not settle what a passage teaches.

### When to apply

When interpreting Old Testament texts whose meaning depends on ANE assumptions:
creation and flood accounts, cosmology, kingship, covenant and treaty language,
temple and sacrifice, law collections, and family or clan structures.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Ask what the original audience assumed** about the cosmos, the gods, kingship,
   and social order, and how those assumptions differ from modern ones.
2. **Read accounts on their own terms.** Consider how ANE creation, flood, and
   kingship texts framed such topics, and what a passage communicates within (or
   against) that frame.
3. **Recognize shared cultural forms** — treaty/covenant structures, temple
   ideology, law-collection styles, genealogies — without assuming the text is
   simply borrowing.
4. **Use comparative literature carefully.** Parallels can illuminate shared
   conventions, but similarity does not prove direct dependence or that meanings
   match; note both likeness and difference.
5. **Attend to family and clan structures** (household, kinship, inheritance,
   land) that shape narrative and law.
6. **Distinguish background from message.** Reconstructed context is a lens for
   understanding, not a substitute for what the text itself says
   (see **Observation, Interpretation, Application**).

### Common errors to avoid

- Reading modern science, cosmology, or individualism into ANE texts.
- Treating a parallel as proof of borrowing, or assuming a shared form carries a
  shared meaning ("parallelomania").
- Asserting specific ANE facts, dates, or reconstructions the evidence does not
  support (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).
- Letting reconstructed background override the text's own emphasis.

### Handling uncertainty

ANE reconstruction is an active, debated field, and comparative claims range from
well-grounded to speculative. Distinguish established background from contested
reconstruction, present alternatives where scholars differ, and label your
confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## Covenant and Treaty Forms

### Purpose

This module helps you recognize covenant language and covenant structures in
Scripture and the ancient treaty forms they often echo. The aim is recognition —
spotting where a text uses covenant categories — not defending any particular
covenant theology or system.

### When to apply

When a text uses covenant vocabulary or structure: oaths and promises, treaty or
covenant ceremonies, stipulations, blessings and curses, signs and seals,
covenant renewal, or language of loyalty and faithfulness between parties.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Identify the covenant elements present** — parties, historical prologue,
   stipulations, blessings and curses, witnesses, signs — and which are emphasized.
2. **Compare with ANE treaty forms** (see **The Ancient Near East**) where
   the structure echoes them, noting both similarity and difference.
3. **Track promises, obligations, and consequences** as the text frames them,
   distinguishing what is stated from what is inferred.
4. **Notice covenant renewal and remembrance** — ceremonies, retellings, and
   appeals that re-invoke an existing covenant.
5. **Read "faithfulness" and "loyalty" language** as covenant terms in context,
   without importing a later technical definition.
6. **Trace covenant themes across texts** through genuine links
   (see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**), while letting each passage speak in its own
   setting.

### Common errors to avoid

- Defending or assuming a particular covenant theology (the various systems that
  organize the biblical covenants) rather than describing the text's own language.
- Forcing every promise or relationship into a formal covenant structure.
- Flattening different covenants in Scripture into one undifferentiated idea.
- Asserting treaty parallels or details the evidence does not support
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

How closely a text follows a treaty form, and how the biblical covenants relate,
are debated and tradition-linked. Present the major readings, separate structure
from interpretation, and label your confidence
(see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave the systematizing to the reader.

## Exile and Restoration

### Purpose

This module supplies the world of the Babylonian exile and the Persian-period
return — the loss of land, temple, and king, life under foreign empires, and the
struggle to rebuild a community and its worship. It helps you read texts shaped by
catastrophe, diaspora, and restoration. It surveys the setting; it does not settle
what a passage teaches.

### When to apply

When interpreting texts that assume or respond to exile and return: the close of
Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Lamentations, much of Jeremiah,
Ezekiel, and Daniel, the later chapters of Isaiah, and the post-exilic prophets
(Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, and others).

### Interpretive moves

1. **Locate the text relative to the exile.** Distinguish warnings before
   judgment, laments and reflection during exile, and the rebuilding and reform of
   the return — each carries different concerns.
2. **Account for empire.** Babylonian and then Persian rule set the conditions for
   deportation, return, and rebuilding; note how imperial power shapes a text's
   situation.
3. **Hear covenant and temple at stake.** Loss and hoped-for restoration of land,
   temple, and community frame these texts (see **Covenant and Treaty Forms**,
   **Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**); restoration is rarely a simple return to the former order.
4. **Attend to diaspora life.** Some texts depict faithful life under foreign
   rule, far from the land, raising questions of identity, assimilation, and hope.
5. **Read restoration soberly.** Return narratives often register
   disappointment and unfinished hope, not triumph; observe how a text frames the
   gap.

### Common errors to avoid

- Confusing the empires and phases (pre-exilic, exilic, Persian-period return).
- Assuming the return undid the exile or restored the monarchy as before.
- Importing modern experiences of nationhood or migration onto the ancient
  setting.
- Asserting specific dates, decrees, or events beyond the evidence
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

The chronology of the return, the composition of the relevant books, and the
historical reconstruction behind them are debated. Distinguish well-attested
background from contested reconstruction, present alternatives where scholars
differ, and label your confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## The Greco-Roman World (Hellenistic Culture)

### Purpose

This module supplies the broader Hellenistic cultural world the New Testament
moved in — its language, education, philosophy, rhetoric, religion, and social
forms. Where **The Roman Empire (First-Century Setting)** foregrounds imperial power and structure,
this module foregrounds the shared Greek-speaking culture that shaped how ideas
were argued, communities formed, and gods were worshipped. It surveys the
setting; it does not settle what a passage teaches.

### When to apply

When interpreting New Testament texts whose meaning engages Greco-Roman culture:
philosophical terms or commonplaces, rhetorical argument, athletic or theatrical
imagery, popular morality, religious cults and temples, voluntary associations,
and the conventions of Greek letters and speech (especially Acts and the
Epistles).

### Interpretive moves

1. **Hear cultural vocabulary.** Terms drawn from philosophy, athletics, the law
   court, or the marketplace carried recognized associations; weigh whether a
   given use engages that background.
2. **Recognize rhetorical conventions.** Greco-Roman speech and letter-writing
   had standard forms of persuasion; note how a text argues, not only what it
   says.
3. **Account for the religious landscape** — many gods, temples, mystery cults,
   and the imperial cult — as the plausibility structure an audience assumed.
4. **Note social forms.** Households, patronage, honor and shame
   (see **Honor and Shame Cultures**), and voluntary associations shaped how early
   communities were organized and perceived.
5. **Weigh engagement, not just parallel.** A shared term or form may be adopted,
   adapted, or contested; observe how the text uses it rather than assuming
   borrowing.

### Common errors to avoid

- Treating any parallel with a philosophy or cult as proof of dependence
  ("parallelomania").
- Flattening the diverse religious and philosophical scene into one "pagan"
  backdrop.
- Importing modern individualism, secularism, or categories of "religion" into
  the ancient setting.
- Asserting specific cultural facts or influences beyond the evidence
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

How strongly a text engages a philosophical school, cult, or rhetorical
convention is often debated. Distinguish well-attested background from contested
reconstruction, present alternatives where scholars differ, and label your
confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## The Israelite Monarchy

### Purpose

This module supplies the world of the Israelite and Judahite monarchy — the rise
of kingship, the united and then divided kingdoms, the royal court, and the
prophets who addressed it. It helps you read texts that assume that political and
religious order without importing modern statecraft. It surveys the setting; it
does not settle what a passage teaches.

### When to apply

When interpreting texts set in or addressed to the monarchy: Samuel, Kings, and
Chronicles; royal and Zion psalms; and the pre-exilic prophets who spoke to kings
and nations.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Place the text on the political map.** Distinguish the united monarchy from
   the later divided kingdoms (Israel in the north, Judah in the south), and note
   which audience and era a passage addresses.
2. **Read kingship in its ancient frame.** Royal ideology, dynasty, and the
   king's role in worship and justice were shaped by the wider ancient Near East
   (see **The Ancient Near East**); weigh both likeness and difference.
3. **Connect king, covenant, and temple.** Promises to the dynasty, the
   Jerusalem temple, and covenant obligations (see **Covenant and Treaty Forms**,
   **Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**) frame how these texts evaluate rulers.
4. **Hear the prophets in their political moment** — addressing specific kings,
   alliances, and crises — rather than as timeless oracles detached from events.
5. **Note differing presentations.** Samuel–Kings and Chronicles narrate the same
   era with different emphases; observe each account's shaping before harmonizing.

### Common errors to avoid

- Reading modern nationhood, democracy, or church–state categories into the
  ancient monarchy.
- Collapsing the northern and southern kingdoms, or confusing their kings and
  timelines.
- Treating royal or Zion language as detached from its political setting.
- Asserting specific dates, regnal details, or reconstructions beyond the
  evidence (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

Chronology, the relationship among the sources, and the history behind the
narratives are actively debated. Distinguish well-attested background from
contested reconstruction, present alternatives where scholars differ, and label
your confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## The Roman Empire (First-Century Setting)

### Purpose

This module supplies the first-century Roman backdrop against which the New
Testament was written. It helps you read the political, social, and economic
realities a first-century audience took for granted — empire, citizenship,
slavery, and imperial ideology — without importing modern assumptions.

### When to apply

When interpreting New Testament texts whose meaning depends on the Roman world:
references to rulers, citizenship, taxation, slavery, courts, travel, military
imagery, or the language of "lord," "savior," and "gospel."

### Interpretive moves

1. **Locate the text in the imperial order.** Consider how Roman power,
   provincial administration, and local rulers shaped daily life for the
   audience.
2. **Hear imperial vocabulary.** Terms such as *gospel* (good news), *lord*,
   *savior*, and *peace* also carried public, imperial meanings; weigh whether a
   given use engages that background.
3. **Account for social structure.** Slavery, the household, citizenship, and
   sharp status differences were pervasive and shape many passages.
4. **Note the mixed setting** of many early communities — Jews and Gentiles,
   citizens and non-citizens, free and enslaved — and how that affects the text.
5. **Connect economy and travel** — roads, trade, patronage, and mobility — to
   the spread of letters and the situations they address.

### Common errors to avoid

- Reading modern nation-state politics or economics into the Roman setting.
- Assuming every use of "lord" or "gospel" is a deliberate anti-imperial
  statement — or that none is; weigh each case.
- Flattening first-century slavery into modern categories without noting the
  differences and the debate.
- Asserting specific historical details that the evidence does not support
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

Much about local conditions and how strongly a text engages imperial ideology is
debated. Distinguish well-established background (consensus) from contested
readings, and label your confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## Second Temple Judaism

### Purpose

This module helps you read the New Testament against the diverse Jewish world of
the Second Temple period (roughly the centuries between the Testaments and into
the first century CE). That world shaped the assumptions of Jesus, Paul, the
Gospels, Acts, and Revelation. The module is descriptive: it surveys the
landscape of beliefs and institutions, not which view is correct.

### When to apply

When interpreting New Testament texts that assume Jewish institutions, debates,
or expectations — synagogue scenes, disputes among groups, messianic language,
resurrection and afterlife, purity and Sabbath questions, and use of Scripture.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Recognize the diversity.** Second Temple Judaism included varied groups
   (such as Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and others) with differing views; do
   not flatten it into a single uniform "Judaism."
2. **Place the text within live debates** of the era — for example, about
   resurrection, law observance, the Temple, and the calendar — without taking
   sides on the theology.
3. **Note messianic expectations were varied,** not singular; describe the range
   rather than assuming one fixed expectation.
4. **Attend to synagogue and Scripture-reading practices,** including how texts
   were read, translated, and interpreted in the period.
5. **Connect to Temple practice and purity** where relevant
   (see **Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence**), and to the Roman setting
   (see **The Roman Empire (First-Century Setting)**).
6. **Trace how a NT author uses Jewish Scripture and traditions**
   (see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**) without assuming later or modern Jewish
   practice mirrors the first century.

### Common errors to avoid

- Treating Second Temple Judaism as monolithic, or projecting later rabbinic
  Judaism back onto the first century.
- Assuming a single, fixed messianic expectation.
- Caricaturing Jewish groups (e.g., as mere foils) rather than describing them.
- Asserting specifics about sects, dates, or practices beyond the evidence
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

Sources for the period are partial and the field is actively debated. Present the
range of reconstructions, separate well-attested practice from inference, and
label your confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## Temple, Sacred Space, and Presence

### Purpose

This module helps you recognize the Temple — and the wider idea of sacred space —
as a major literary and historical thread running through Scripture. It supplies
the institutional background (priesthood, sacrifice, holiness, purity, divine
presence) and flags a recurring sacred-space motif, so you can see how a passage
draws on these ideas. It describes the role of the Temple; it does not prescribe
a theology of it.

### When to apply

When interpreting texts involving the tabernacle or temple, priesthood,
sacrifice and offerings, holiness and purity, festivals, or language about God's
presence "dwelling" with people.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Identify the institution in view** — tabernacle, first or second temple,
   priesthood, sacrificial system, festival, or purity regulation — and its
   function in its setting.
2. **Read sacred space as ordered around presence and holiness:** graded zones of
   access, the role of priests as mediators, and purity as fitness for that
   space.
3. **Understand sacrifice and purity within their own system** before drawing
   analogies; describe what an offering or rite did in context.
4. **Recognize the recurring sacred-space motif** that many readers trace across
   Scripture (for example, Eden, tabernacle, temple, and new-creation imagery).
   Present it as a literary theme to notice and trace through real textual links
   (see **Intertextuality and Scriptural Connections**) — not as a settled doctrinal scheme.
5. **Let the ANE background inform temple ideology** where relevant
   (see **The Ancient Near East**).
6. **Distinguish the historical institution from later interpretation** of it,
   keeping observation separate from theological conclusions.

### Common errors to avoid

- Collapsing distinct institutions (tabernacle, temples, synagogue) into one.
- Presenting one tradition's theology of temple or sacrifice as the plain meaning.
- Treating the Eden-to-new-creation motif as a proven doctrinal system rather than
  a literary theme that must be supported text by text.
- Asserting details of ritual, architecture, or dates beyond the evidence
  (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

Details of practice and the strength of thematic connections are debated. Note
which links are explicit and which are interpretive, present alternatives, and
label your confidence (see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**).

## The Wisdom Tradition

### Purpose

This module supplies the intellectual world of the biblical wisdom tradition — a
way of reflecting on order, justice, suffering, and how to live well, shared
across the ancient Near East. It helps you read wisdom texts within that
conversation, including its internal debates. It surveys the setting; it does not
settle what a passage concludes. For how the genre communicates, see
**Wisdom Genre Module**.

### When to apply

When interpreting wisdom material — Proverbs, Job, and Ecclesiastes especially,
and wisdom influence elsewhere (some psalms, and in the New Testament, James) —
particularly where a text reflects on consequence, suffering, the limits of
knowing, or the fear of the LORD.

### Interpretive moves

1. **Hear the tradition's conversation.** Wisdom books address shared questions —
   does the world reward the righteous? why do the innocent suffer? — and
   sometimes answer one another; read a text as part of that exchange.
2. **Distinguish the modes.** Practical wisdom (Proverbs) states general patterns;
   reflective or "skeptical" wisdom (Job, Ecclesiastes) probes their limits. Hold
   them together rather than flattening one into the other.
3. **Read proverbs as observed tendencies,** not unconditional promises or laws.
4. **Situate it in the ancient Near East** (see **The Ancient Near East**),
   where comparable wisdom literature circulated; note both likeness and
   distinctive features, without assuming borrowing.
5. **Respect tensions as deliberate.** Apparent contradictions within and between
   wisdom books are often part of the tradition's argument, not errors to resolve
   away.

### Common errors to avoid

- Reading proverbs as guaranteed outcomes and then treating exceptions as
  failures.
- Forcing Job or Ecclesiastes into tidy resolutions that blunt their questions.
- Treating parallels with other ANE wisdom as proof of dependence.
- Asserting specifics about authorship, date, or social setting beyond the
  evidence (see **Anti-Hallucination and Sourcing Discipline**).

### Handling uncertainty

The dating, social setting, and interrelation of the wisdom books are debated,
and these texts deliberately leave hard questions open. Present the range,
distinguish observation from reconstruction, and label your confidence
(see **Epistemic Humility and Confidence Labels**); leave conclusions to the reader.
